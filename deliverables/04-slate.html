2. The tournament in one view

Method from D-015. The showrunner wrote 20 concepts, 5 per slot. Three judge agents scored every concept on five criteria, each 1 to 10, equal weight, maximum 50. A script aggregated the scores. The top 2 per slot went to a head-to-head. One winner per slot. No override was used (D-020).

The three judge lenses

  • Judge K, the kid. Reads every concept as an 11-year-old and as a 7-year-old, and asks whether each age has something to watch.
  • Judge P, the parent. Born 1985, kids 7 and 11. Asks whether the pitch is funny on the page to an adult and whether the house would sit down for it.
  • Judge X, the producer. Plans AI-assisted trailers first and cheap full episodes after. Asks what the set, cast, and consistency problems cost.

Criteria: engine (does it yield 100 episodes), hook (the two-generation pull), originality (distance from any existing show), trailer (strength of the six-beat teaser), standards (fit with the content rules). A concept is disqualified if any judge scores standards below 5 or if its engine does not yield 100 episodes. None was disqualified.

Slot 1, Anchor
Kitchen Court

Logline. In a house where Mom is a real judge, every family fight goes to trial at the kitchen table, and when the kids sue their parents, a kid presides.

Pitch. It's about a family where the mom is an actual small claims judge, so every fight in the house, a waffle, a bedtime, whose turn it is, goes to trial at the kitchen table. The grandma is a retired basketball referee, so she doesn't decide anything, she just blows a whistle and calls fouls. The six-year-old is the bailiff and she runs the toaster, and when your toast pops, your time is up. And the house rule is that when the kids sue a grown-up, a kid gets to be the judge. Every verdict ends with a snack and a hug, no exceptions. It's the only show where a first-grader can overrule a judge and the judge has to serve her a snack.

The engine

Every episode has one A-case governed by the five Fridge Rules: anything over five dollars or five minutes goes to the table; each side gets one slice of toast and stops when it pops; evidence must fit on the table; when a grown-up is a party, the next kid on the Rota presides; and no verdict is final until the loser picks the snack, the winner serves it, both hug, and Ref whistles. Every episode also has a B-story from one of four streams (Wendell's rental inventory, Renata's downtown world leaking home, the Pinwheel neighbors, the kids' week) and the B-story must collide with the A-case at the verdict. Stakes rise across a season without leaving the kitchen: waffle, bedtime, hoodie, whether the court takes outside cases, whether Mom takes a bigger court. Seven streams multiply; the bible lists 100 seeds and the showrunner estimates a room could write 200.

Season 1 arc, ten episodes

Season question: who decides? Can Renata be fair to people she loves, and can a kid be the judge for real?

  1. The Last Waffle. Pip files against Ansel, Renata's first ruling against her own child runs eleven pages, and Ref invents delay of game.
  2. Counsel. Marisol files for a later bedtime and hires Ref as her lawyer, and Ref hates every second and wins.
  3. Six Chairs. Wendell rents out all forty chairs, Ansel files Family v. Facilities, and Rule Four puts Pip on the Stool for her first grown-up case.
  4. Aisle Six. A litigant recognizes Renata at the grocery store, and Ansel finds the word "precedent" and relitigates last year.
  5. The Back Door. A neighbor kid brings the first outside case, and the Full Table outvotes Renata six forks to one.
  6. Order. Ref's whistle goes missing, the court dissolves into a normal family argument, and the whistle turns up in the freezer, filed as evidence by Pip.
  7. Across the River. Renata is asked to sit on a bigger court, tells only Wendell, and Wendell writes it on a docket slip by accident.
  8. The Binder. The kids draft a Household Constitution that is better than the Fridge Rules, and Ref refuses to sign anything.
  9. Everyone v. Mom, part one. The kids file to block the new job, the Rota says Pip presides, and the trial cannot start because nobody is hungry.
  10. Everyone v. Mom, part two. Pip rules from the second step: Mom takes the job, Kitchen Court moves to breakfast, and Ref uses the full whistle once.

Long Route

Logline. Six kids ride the county's last long school bus route for 48 minutes twice a day, and the bus is where everything that happens to them actually happens.

Pitch. There is a school bus that goes all the way out to the orchards and back. Six kids ride it, and they have turned it into a tiny country: assigned seats, a written code, a back-row parliament, and a market for the good window. The ride is the only place these six are all together, and they will never admit it is the best part of the day.

The engine

Every episode is a ride, morning or afternoon or both, and the story moves in stop order so the viewer always knows where on the route it is by what is outside the window. The back row runs a parliament with a Speaker, a whip, a clerk, and Priya's written code, and every episode has one session about the thing the episode is really about; the code forbids motions about a person. One stop per episode brings the outside in: a face boards, a rule bends, a sign changes. The seat economy gives every ride a small stake, with Cal's ledger, Priya's code, and Frances's veto. Against the sameness risk the judges named, the bible lists ten levers: windows, the loop, stops, weather, substitutes, breakdowns, field trips, route changes, seat changes, and ride direction. Two rides a day, six kids, eight stops, five seasons of road, and one driver with a life multiply to 100 without strain.

Season 1 arc, ten episodes

Season question: does the Long Route survive the year? The county has a limit on route length, and Route 12 runs nine minutes over.

  2. The Quiet. The twins split on a vote for the first time, and the fight has to be held in silence at the rail crossing.
  3. Position Six. The bus is moved in the loop, and the six campaign through the door to get Position 3 back.
  4. Harvest Seats. Frances reassigns seats for orchard season. Cal's ledger collapses.

  7. Chains. First snow. The ride runs double. Parliament passes eleven motions and repeals ten. Mr. Tull rode a long route once too.

  9. Ines. Mateo's sister gets a trial ride. The county proposes a van for Pennock Hollow. Mateo would ride alone, and says he wants that.
  10. Board Meeting on Wheels. The county board rides the route. Mateo says out loud that it is not a long route, it is the whole way. The route stays.

Recent History Society

Logline. A neighborhood historical society reenacts only what happened last week, in full costume, with total seriousness, and a grandfather and his granddaughter run it.

Pitch. There is a club in a brick hall that treats last Tuesday like ancient history. The grocery store ran out of eggs, so twelve people in bonnets and hats stage the empty shelf as a fallen empire, with a narrator, a printed program, and a panel of historians who argue about which aisle it was. The engine

A twelve-minute episode with a fixed spine: the Fall (a slow-motion tumble in costume with no context), the Call to Order, the Reenactment, the Panel, the Field Piece at the real site, and the Corrections read into the record. The one joke is that people treat the trivial as history, and D-020 required it to escalate by rule. It does so on two clocks. Inside an episode, the Restaging Ladder (Article 7): every sustained objection restages the scene bigger and keeps everything that was already wrong. Across a season, the Scale Ladder: the kind of event climbs seven rungs, from ordinary events to smaller events, disputed events, events nobody saw, the Society itself, an event too big, and an event during the reenactment, and the season never stays on one rung for more than two episodes. Four inputs multiply: the event, the casting (no member plays themselves), the dispute, and the field. The heart is Harlan and Piper, who take each other seriously, always.

The Returns Desk

Logline. At the all-night returns desk of a neighborhood hardware store, every item comes back with a ghost story, and the desk always finds the ordinary truth.

Pitch. It is a hardware store that never closes. People bring things back at night, and every one of them swears the thing is haunted. The woman at the returns desk believes all of them and files the paperwork anyway. The owner's twelve-year-old, doing homework at the counter, figures out what really happened. It is a little spooky, then it is funny, every single time, and nobody on the show is ever in real danger.

The engine

An eighteen-minute anthology inside a frame. The frame is the desk. The anthology is the customer's story: one item, one customer, one set, one insert location per episode. The item does its one strange thing on screen, small. Marguerite takes the story and fills out the form. Ines asks three questions. The insert plays the customer's version at night with every fair clue in focus. Teddy's own small scare resolves first so the youngest viewer sees a safe landing before the main one. Then the fair reveal: Ines names the cause, the insert replays the exact shot with the clue lit, and no new information is added. Three rules guard against cheats: write the cause first, put the clue on screen, and replay rather than reveal. The one-thing rule caps every scare: an object does exactly one unexplained thing and never adds a second. Ten item categories by ten cause categories give a hundred cells, and no cell is used twice.
