1. Position

Source key: A is canon/AUDIENCE.md. G is deliverables/01-gap-memo.html. Section numbers follow each fact. No other sources are used in this section.

One line. An original comedy block for the whole house, on one fixed night a week, made new for the kids of the parents who grew up on one.

For whom

The household. One parent account plus the people who watch with it (A, section 1).

The parent

  • Born 1981 to 1990. Age 36 to 45 in 2026 (A, section 2).
  • Watched a fixed-night family comedy block as a child. Recalls the night, the hosts, the late bedtime, and the jingle more than any one plot (A, section 2).
  • Already co-views. Over 90 percent of parents of children 2 to 12 co-view (A, section 1).
  • Feels behind on screen time. 42 percent say they could manage it better. 65 percent say they spend too much time on their own phone (A, section 2).
  • Names nostalgia as a driver of shared content time (A, section 2).
  • Holds the account, the remote, and the decision about the night (A, section 2).

The child

  • Age 6 to 14, in two bands. Ages 6 to 9 watch with a parent almost always. Ages 10 to 14 start to pull toward feeds and games (A, section 3).
  • Wants something that feels like theirs, characters to talk about at school, and a mild scare with a safe landing (A, section 3).
  • Cannot hold an account, comment, chat, or receive a notification. The product never asks the child for anything (A, section 3).

The parent's jobs the brand must serve (A, section 2)

  1. One night a week where the whole family sits on one couch and laughs at the same thing.
  2. A screen ritual the parent is proud of, not one the parent apologizes for.
  3. A way to share a piece of the parent's own childhood without showing the kid a 30-year-old rerun.
  4. Make it easy. Tell me when, tell me what, and give me something to do after.

Against what

The brand competes for a night, not for a library.

Why now

  1. The audience math lines up. 33.3 million US families have children under 18. Parents of today's 6 to 14 year olds were born mostly in the 1980s. That is the generation that watched a fixed-night comedy block as children. G, section 1, H3a; A, section 2.
  2. The supply gap is real. No US streamer, FAST service, kids network, creator studio, or faith network runs a hosted, original, scripted comedy block for families on a fixed night. G, section 1, H1; G, section 3.
  3. The old blocks ended on cost and strategy, not on the end of demand. G, section 1, H4.
  4. Kids media is contracting. One kids cable channel went dark in March 2025. One family cable channel closed in August 2025. The largest family streamer retired watch-together in 2023. Fewer incumbents hold the night. G, section 3.
  5. Appointment viewing survives on streaming. Weekly drops create watercooler moments. G, section 2, H1.
  6. Made-for-kids platform rules disable comments, notifications, and playlists. The weekly reminder must live in a parent-facing channel. That makes the owned site the product, not a marketing page. G, section 4; G, section 9.
  7. A small studio can prove audience before capital. Nine creator cases show it. G, section 1, H5.

What the brand must not claim

  • It must not claim that a 30-year nostalgia cycle drives timing. That hypothesis is not supported (G, section 1, H3b).
  • It must not claim parent demand for a weekly ritual until the demand test measures it (A, section 5).
  • It must not claim to serve ages 13 to 14 with co-viewing data. No source measures that band (A, section 2).

Position statement

For parents born in the 1980s who want one night a week on one couch with their kids, [NAME] is the original comedy block that premieres on a fixed night, with new characters for today's 6 to 14 year olds and the ritual the parent remembers. Unlike the feed, the library, and the rerun, it owns a night, not a scroll.

[NAME] is the provisional name constant until Fork D closes and counsel clears CG-01.

Recommendation: Streetlights. COUNSEL_GATE CG-01 stays open.

Streetlights is the recommendation because it is the only name of the five with no trademark hit in entertainment, video, or family content, and it carries the exact memory the parent persona holds. AUDIENCE.md section 2 says the parent was born 1981 to 1990 and recalls the night more than any plot. That parent also remembers the rule of that era: come home when the streetlights come on. The name turns the old signal to go inside into the new signal to sit down together. It is one word, two syllables, and a kid can chant it. It names a light, an evening, and a neighborhood without naming a porch. On a printed guide masthead it reads like a real publication. The domain field is the cost: the .com belongs to a real estate developer, so the site would live on the .tv or a phrase domain, and the handles would need a modifier such as a "night" or "watch" prefix, both unchecked. Rumpus is the runner-up and moves to first if counsel finds the prior kids entertainment mark dead and clears the pet-toy and font marks as non-confusing. Pilot Light, Screen Door, and Lights Up each collide with a working company in the same field and should not go to counsel unless the first two fail.

Counsel clears the trademark before any filing, any public use, or any domain purchase beyond a defensive hold. Held in reserve if all five fail: Lamppost, Hall Light, Gloaming, Homeglow, Evenglow.

4. Voice

Voice in one line. A confident host who is glad you came. Warm, plain, funny on purpose, never winking.

The ten rules

  1. Speak to the parent. Never call a child to act. No "click," "subscribe," "share," or "tell us" aimed at a kid (canon/STANDARDS.md section 2, item 10).
  2. Short sentences. One idea each. If a sentence needs a comma to breathe, split it.
  3. Say the night. Every public page names the fixed night and the block. The reader should never wonder when.
  4. Be warm without being soft. No "cozy," "snuggle," "magical," or "wholesome." Show the couch. Do not describe the feeling of the couch.
  5. Be confident without being loud. No exclamation points in headings. One per page at most in body copy.
  6. Never wink. No irony about being a TV block, no "remember when," no quotation marks around the word retro. The guide format is played straight.
  7. Never name the old blocks. No use of the two block trademarks named in canon/STANDARDS.md section 2, item 13, and no reference to any 1990s brand, jingle, or catchphrase in any public text (section 2, items 12 and 13).
  8. Jokes land for a 35-year-old and stay clean for a 6-year-old. If a line needs the kid not to get it, cut it.
  9. State facts with a source or not at all. Audience numbers, results, and claims about the old blocks stay in research files.
  10. No em dashes. Restructure the sentence. Spell out acronyms on first use.

5. Identity

The idea. The site is a printed TV guide that came to life. A family opens it, finds the night, reads the four listings, and clicks one to watch the trailer. It must feel like a real publication with a real art director, not a costume. The 1990s reference stays in the bones (grid, type, numbers, paper) and never in the surface (no scan lines, no video-tape noise, no fake static, no "retro" badge).

Premium means tight typography, generous margins, real hierarchy, one accent, and motion that stays out of the way. Kitsch means glow filters, neon, distressed textures, and jokes about the format. The brief bans the second list.

Rules for both

  • Two families per page. No third.
  • No faux bold, no faux small caps. Use the real small-caps feature only where the face supports it; otherwise set capitals at 80 percent size with 5 percent tracking.
  • Minimum body size 16 pixels. Minimum listing blurb 15 pixels. Parents read on phones at night.

Color system

One paper, one ink, one accent, one on-air glow. Grays derive from ink at fixed opacities. No gradients except the glow halo.

Light (default, "daytime edition")

Paper#F3ECDD Page background. Warm cream, no texture.
Ink#1C1A16 All text, rules, numerals. Ink 60 and Ink 20 are this value at 60 and 20 percent opacity.
Accent#C9391E One per page: the premiere rule, the primary button, the active link. A brick red that prints.
On-air glow#FFC46B The now-playing dot, the play halo, the sonic logo visual. 100 percent as a dot, 30 percent as a halo.

Dark ("late edition")

Paper#15130F Page background. Ink-black with warmth.
Ink#EFE6D3 All text, rules, numerals. Ink 60 and Ink 20 at the same opacities.
Accent#E8583A Same roles. Lifted for contrast.
On-air glow#FFD08A Same roles. Brighter on dark.
  • Contrast: ink on paper passes WCAG AA at every size in both modes. Accent on paper passes AA at 18 pixels and up. Never set body text in accent.
  • The glow is the only thing on the page that looks lit. It appears only where something is playing or about to play.
  • The trailer pages use the dark set by default, so the video is the brightest object.
  • Slot 4, Lights Out, may use the dark set on its listing row in the light edition. That is the one exception to a single paper per page.

Motion rules

What movesWhat never moves
  1. The on-air glow breathes. Opacity 30 to 60 percent over a slow cycle. It is the only ambient motion on the site.
  2. Listings reveal on load in slot order, first to last, each with a 40 millisecond stagger and a 6 pixel rise. One pass. Never on scroll.
  3. The trailer player fades from paper to dark when play starts. The listing behind it stays put.
  4. The vote button confirms with a single ink fill from left to right and a checkmark. No confetti, no bounce.
  5. Hover on a listing raises the hairline rule to ink 60. Nothing else.
  1. The masthead. It is print. It does not scroll away, shrink, or animate.
  2. Type. No kinetic type, no letter-by-letter reveals, no marquee.
  3. The grid. Columns do not shift on hover or reflow on interaction.
  4. The paper. No parallax, no grain, no flicker, no scan lines, no static.
  5. Anything for longer than 300 milliseconds, except the glow breath.

Accessibility. Honor prefers-reduced-motion. When set, the glow holds at 45 percent and the load stagger is removed. No strobe of any kind (canon/STANDARDS.md section 3, item 9).

Direction 1, recommended
The Masthead

The name set in the condensed display face, full width, all capitals, tracked tight. Under it, a single hairline rule and the fixed night in small caps. The mark is typography alone. The distinguishing detail is a single amber dot (the on-air glow) placed as the tittle of one letter or as a period after the name. In the favicon, the dot alone on paper is the mark.

Strength: reads as a real publication. Cheapest to build. Ages well.
Risk: without the dot it could be any masthead. The dot must be consistent.

Direction 2
The Signal

A wordmark plus a small geometric symbol: a lit window. A rectangle in ink with a smaller rectangle in amber inside it, offset to the upper right, like a lamp seen through a window from the street. Nothing else. The symbol sits left of the name at masthead size and stands alone as the favicon and the app tile. It animates once on load: the inner rectangle goes from paper to amber.

Strength: gives the brand an object a kid can draw. Works in motion. Ties to the glow.
Risk: window symbols exist in other categories. Counsel checks the symbol as well as the word.

Direction 3
The Number

The name set in the display face with the four slot numerals 1 2 3 4 set beneath it in the same face, spaced to the width of the name, with a hairline rule between. The numerals are the promise: four shows, in order, every week. The favicon is the numeral 4 in ink with the amber dot.

Strength: says the format without a tagline. Distinct from any single-show brand.
Risk: if the format changes slot count, the mark changes. Fits only if the format architect fixes four slots as canon.

Recommendation: Direction 1 for the site launch, with the amber dot as the constant, and Direction 2 held for the app tile and video platform channel art once the format is fixed.

The not-slop checklist

The site must pass all ten before the standards agent reviews it. A page that fails any one item goes back to the builder.

  1. Every image on the site was made for the site. No stock photography, no generic AI render of a family on a couch, no clip art. If the site has a photo, it has a credit.
  2. Every word on the site was written by a person and read aloud once. No filler paragraphs, no "Welcome to our website," no placeholder text.
  3. No page has more than one accent color element competing for the eye. Count them. One per view.
  4. The masthead, the listing grid, and the footer are identical on every page. A returning parent knows where they are in one glance.
  5. Zero texture overlays. No grain, no paper fiber image, no scan lines, no vignette. Warmth comes from the hex values and the type.
  6. Type is never stretched, skewed, or faux-bolded. Font widths come from the variable axis only.
  7. Every trailer page shows the same four things in the same order: the listing line, the player, the vote, the parent note. No page adds a fifth thing.
  8. Load time: the guide page renders its type and grid before any video asset loads. A parent on a phone sees listings in the first paint.
  9. Nothing on the site addresses a child. Read every button and heading as if a 7-year-old were the reader. If it asks them to do anything, rewrite it (canon/STANDARDS.md section 2, item 10).
  10. The site says, on every page, that a parent holds the account and that no child data is collected (canon/STANDARDS.md section 4, item 2). The footer line in the voice section is the default.
