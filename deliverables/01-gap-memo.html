Gap Memo: Go, Pivot, or Kill

This memo closes Phase 1. It tests five hypotheses with sourced evidence, maps the landscape, sizes the market within bounds, scores the format mechanics, and answers the red team's eight high-severity attacks. It ends with the two decisions the owner must make: Fork A (go, pivot, or kill) and Fork B (primary distribution path).

Fork A recommendation
GO to a demand test, not to production

Neither kill hypothesis failed. Neither is fully confirmed. H1 is partial: the supply gap is real in narrow form, and demand is untested. H5 holds at medium confidence. Proceed to Phase 2 and Phase 3. Run the demand smoke test before any v0 content spend. Ratify the kill thresholds below.

What changed after the red team
The gap is narrower and the bet is sharper

A public broadcaster already runs a weekly Friday family movie night. Three streaming-era nostalgia sitcoms drew both generations on night one and lost the child by season three. The wrapper raises the floor. The show keeps the child. Retention is the whole test.

Fork B recommendation
Owned parent site first. Video platform as a feed, not a home.

No channel in the landscape keeps every block mechanic alive. The owned site is the only surface where the reminder, the premiere, and the return measurement all work. The video platform brings discovery under made-for-kids limits. FAST is the scale path after proof.

2. Evidence by hypothesis

H1. Appointment co-viewing is underserved

  • Americans watch TV with other people 47 percent of the time, on linear and on connected TV alike. Nielsen, "What is co-viewing", 2024.
  • Over 90 percent of parents of children 2 to 12 co-view. The rate is 94 percent for ages 6 to 9. No separate figure exists for ages 10 to 12, and ages 13 to 14 were not surveyed. Daily family co-viewing holds at 44 to 50 percent. Precise TV and Giraffe Insights via MediaPost, 2024; Precise TV 2024 edition.
  • 73 percent of parents say their kids co-view at least half the time. Kids Industries, 5,000 families in 10 countries, 2023.
  • Appointment viewing survives on streaming. The Christmas 2025 NFL game on Netflix averaged 27.5 million US viewers. Weekly drops create watercooler moments. NFL.com, December 2025; Variety, September 2026.
  • The public broadcaster's family night: every Friday 7 to 9 p.m. since April 2017, encores Saturday and Sunday, movies and specials from existing preschool and early-elementary shows, no hosts. It proves a station will program a family night. It does not serve 6 to 14 with original comedy. PBS KIDS for Parents; Arizona PBS, 2020.
  • A university network streams a free, ad-free family sketch comedy weekly before a studio audience, with 3.45 million YouTube subscribers. It proves free weekly clean comedy holds an audience. It is not a block and not aimed at 6 to 14. PR Newswire, 2026.
  • The largest streamer for families retired its watch-together feature in 2023. Feature removal is evidence of low use of that feature, not of unmet demand for a ritual.

Reading. The supply gap is confirmed in narrow form. The demand half is untested. Section 8 sets the test.

H2. Mechanics made the blocks work

  • Two family sitcoms fell after leaving the Friday broadcast block for a rival's clone in 1997. Both were canceled after one season. One other show posted its best season after leaving the block for a Tuesday slot. Deseret News, 1997; The TV Ratings Guide.
  • Adjacent prime-time shares correlate at 0.49. Lead-in rating and program type explain 96 percent of audience duplication. Tiedge and Ksobiech, Journal of Communication, 1986; Webster, JOBEM, 2006.
  • Block tactics raised renewal rates, not ratings, across 80 percent of new programs. Adams, Journalism and Mass Communication Quarterly, 1997.
  • The 2018 broadcast revival reused existing comedies with no hosts, no interstitials, and minimal promotion. Both comedies hit series lows in week one. It tested a label, not the mechanics. Deadline, October 2018; TheWrap, May 2019.

Reading. The wrapper raises the floor and builds loyalty. The shows set the ceiling. Weight shifts toward Phase 4 (the slate) and toward retention as the primary metric.

H3. Two generations

  • 33.3 million US families had own children under 18 in 2024. The school-age population (5 to 17) is 53.3 million. Even-spread arithmetic gives 36.9 million children age 6 to 14. The even-spread assumption is unsourced and flagged. Census FM-1 via FRED; Census Vintage 2024.
  • Mean age of mothers at first birth rose from 26.6 (2016) to 27.5 (2023). Parents of children born 2012 to 2020 were born mostly 1977 to 1994. The cohort skews older than the master prompt's window because fathers are older and many of these children are second or third births. Births 1981 to 1990 fit the core. CDC NCHS, 2025.
  • 55 percent of Millennial women were mothers by 2018, versus 62 percent of Gen X women at the same age. Pew Research Center, 2020.
  • The 30-year and 40-year nostalgia rules rest on anecdote. The 30-year proponent's own data averages about 23 years. Slate, 2012; The Patterning, 2017.

3. Landscape

The gap, stated narrowly: no US streamer, FAST service, kids network, creator studio, or faith network runs a hosted, original, scripted comedy block for families with children 6 to 14 on a fixed night. The closest live models: the public broadcaster's Friday family movie night; a faith-and-family cable network that premieres every movie Saturday at 8 p.m. ET; a greeting-card network's weekend premiere nights; a values-based studio that releases an animated series every Wednesday to 2.61 million paying members, who supply 81 percent of its revenue. PBS KIDS; Variety 2025; TV Insider 2026; Business Wire Q2 2026.

Kids media is contracting. One kids cable channel went dark in March 2025. One family cable channel closed in August 2025. One ad-supported service folded into its parent. One kids FAST operator faces receivership. The largest family streamer retired watch-together in 2023. landscape.md, sections 2, 3, and 5.

4. Constraints that shape the product

  • Made-for-kids rules. The designation disables comments, notifications, live chat, end screens, cards, playlists, Watch Later, posts, memberships, and personalized ads. Monetization drops to contextual ads. A major studio paid $10 million in 2025 for mislabeling child-directed videos, so "not made for kids" is not a workaround. FTC 2019 settlement; YouTube Help; FTC 2025.
  • COPPA amendments. Effective June 2025, compliance due April 2026. Biometrics count as personal information. Separate consent for non-integral disclosures. Written security and retention programs.
  • Platform structure. FAST channels loop all day and the platform owns the guide slot. Subscription buyers order batches, not schedules. No streamer runs a family watch-together feature.
  • Consequence. The weekly reminder cannot rely on a platform notification. It must live in a parent-facing channel: email, an owned site, or an app with a parent account. This is the core of the Phase 5 product.

Recommendation: Go to a demand test. Ratify these thresholds (D-009).
  1. Demand smoke test before any v0 content spend: a parent landing page with the promise and the fixed night. Floor: 500 parent household sign-ups from organic and low-cost traffic. Miss the floor, and the project pivots to one show or stops.
  2. Proof of audience at the Phase 8 memo: at least 1,000 parent households watch block 1, and at least 35 percent of them watch block 4. Miss either line, and the project stops or pivots. No momentum.
  3. Cost ceiling for v0 content plus product: a dollar figure the owner sets at Fork A. It defines "low cost" for H5.
  4. The slate is the ceiling. Phase 4 names a lead show. v0 leads with it plus the minimum wrapper: fixed night, a recorded host wrap, a sonic ID, one interstitial. The other slots can start as shorter formats.

10. What the next phases must prove

  1. Phase 2. A name that clears trademark and echoes no existing block. Counsel gate CG-01. The landing page for the demand smoke test.
  2. Phase 3. A wrapper that works with a single lead show and scales to four. Reproduce the fixed night. Adapt the home base set, four slots, interstitials, seasonal stunts, sonic ID, and fixed time. Test hosts and crossovers. Drop viewer participation and the stay-up-late framing for v1.
  3. Phase 4. A lead show with original characters for today's 6 to 14 year olds that passes the 100-episode engine test and reads funny to a 35-year-old on the page.
  4. Phase 5. A parent-only product with a week 4 household return rate, measured without any child data, against the D-009 lines.
