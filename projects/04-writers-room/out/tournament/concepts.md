# Tournament Round 1: 20 Concepts

Showrunner output for decision D-015. Five concepts per slot. Judges score. The showrunner does not rank.

Rules applied to every concept:
- Original expression only. No character, set, prop, song, catchphrase, or plot from any existing show. No parody of a real show. No real person.
- Safe for age 6. Interesting for age 35.
- Two-generation test: the parent laughs at something the kid does not fully get. The kid leans into something the parent finds sweet.
- Every concept passes `canon/STANDARDS.md` section 2 on the showrunner's own read. The nearest item is named at the end of each concept.
- Comps to existing shows by title are not used. Only mechanics are described.

Slots:
- Slot 1, Anchor (A1 to A5): multi-generation family sitcom.
- Slot 2, Hangout (H1 to H5): tween and teen ensemble comedy.
- Slot 3, Wildcard (W1 to W5): sketch, variety, or odd animated comedy.
- Slot 4, Lights Out (L1 to L5): mild spooky anthology. Scares resolve with safety and humor.

## A1. Stop Fourteen

**Logline.** Three generations of the Okonkwo-Reyes family live in the only house in town whose front porch is a legal city bus stop, and the whole town waits there.

**World.** Halbrook is a river town of 40,000 with one bus line. In 1987 the city put Stop 14 on the front porch of a corner house because the sidewalk was too narrow, and nobody ever moved the sign. The Okonkwo-Reyes family bought the house last year without reading the easement. Every 22 minutes a bus stops, and whoever is waiting has been sitting on their porch furniture, drinking their coffee, and hearing their arguments through the screen door. The house is the family's home. The porch belongs to everyone.

**Characters.**
- **Teodoro "Teo" Reyes, 71.** Grandfather. Retired driver of this exact bus route. Want: to be useful and to know everything that happens in town. Flaw: he treats the porch as his own broadcast studio and cannot let a stranger sit in silence. Line: "You're on my porch. That means you're on my show. Name and destination."
- **Ijeoma Okonkwo-Reyes, 41.** Mother. City zoning clerk, in the very department that could move the sign. Want: one quiet hour and a porch that is hers. Flaw: she refuses to use her job to fix her own problem, because that would be a conflict of interest, and she is proud of it. Line: "I could fix this with one form. I will not. That is the difference between me and everyone else at city hall."
- **Bram Okonkwo-Reyes, 9.** Youngest child. Want: to be the official Stop Manager, with a clipboard and a whistle. Flaw: he adopts every rider's problem as his personal case. Line: "Ma'am, you've been late three Tuesdays in a row. I have notes."
- Also in the house: Nnamdi Okonkwo-Reyes, 43, father, pediatric dental hygienist. Ada, 14, oldest child, who pretends the porch does not exist.

**Engine.** The porch is a public stage with a fixed timetable. Every 22 minutes new people arrive, and the family cannot leave. Each episode crosses one rider's problem with one family problem. Sample seeds:
1. Teo starts a porch loyalty program for regular riders. Ijeoma discovers he has been giving away her coffee for a month.
2. Bram loses a rider's umbrella and starts a lost and found that takes over the living room.
3. A tour bus stops by mistake and 40 tourists believe the house is a museum. Teo gives the tour.
4. Ijeoma's boss schedules a site inspection of Stop 14. The family must look like they do not live there.
5. Ada's first crush boards at 7:42 every morning. Breakfast is now timed to the second.
6. A snowstorm strands six riders in the house overnight. Teo runs it like a shelter with a talent show.
7. Bram wins Student of the Month for civic service. The plaque is bolted to the porch as public property.
8. A new express route skips Stop 14. The family fights to keep it, then realizes they wanted it gone.
9. Nnamdi tries to work from home on the porch and ends up giving flossing lectures to the 9:05 crowd.
10. The city finally offers to move the sign. The family votes. Teo cheats.

Engine yields 100: yes. Two inputs cross every week. Who walks up the steps is unlimited. What the family is going through is a standard family engine. Neither runs out.

**Two-generation hook.** The parent gets a zoning clerk who will not fix her own problem on principle, a house with a public easement, and the feeling of never being off duty. The kid gets Bram's clipboard authority, Teo's porch show, and a new stranger every week.

**Tone and comedy source.** Character first. Teo's need to host, Ijeoma's principle, Bram's officiousness, Ada's denial. The situation supplies the guests, but the jokes come from how each family member handles a stranger on the furniture. Riders are never the butt of the joke. They are the audience for the family, and the family performs whether it wants to or not. The rhythm is fast, warm, and built on overlapping talk through a screen door.

**Trailer hook (90 seconds, 4 beats).**
1. Morning. The family at breakfast. A bus hisses outside. Six strangers turn and wave through the window. The family waves back, exhausted.
2. Teo on the porch with a spatula as a microphone, interviewing a nurse on her way to work.
3. Ijeoma at city hall, staring at the one form that would end it. She puts it back in the drawer.
4. Night. The last bus passes. The family sits on the empty porch. Bram: "Stop's closed." Teo: "Stop's never closed."

Sample dialogue. Nurse: "I just want to catch the 7:10." Teo: "And I want to know what you think of the new mayor. We can both get what we want."

**Production note.** Live action on one set: the porch, with the kitchen visible through a window and screen door. AI-assisted production does well with the porch exterior at different times of day, bus passes, and crowd extras. It does not yet do four-person overlapping dialogue with timing, so the trailer leans on short exchanges and reaction shots.

**Risk.**
- IP distance: it might be mistaken for a generic porch-and-family sitcom of the 1990s window. It is not that. No existing show has a public bus stop on a porch, a zoning-clerk mother, or a retired driver hosting the stop. No character maps to a named show.
- Standards: nearest is 2.6, strangers on a porch with a 9-year-old. Rule: an adult family member is always on the porch in every scene with a rider. Second nearest is 3.2, Ada's sarcasm must land as affection.
- Biggest creative risk: the porch gimmick reads as a sketch. Mitigation: the family story drives every episode; the porch supplies texture, not plot.

Standards section 2 self-check: pass.

## A2. Kitchen Court

**Logline.** In the Delacroix-Huang house every family dispute goes to trial at the kitchen table, because Mom is a real small claims judge and Grandma is a retired referee.

**World.** A split-level in Verano, a suburb with too many cul-de-sacs. Renata Delacroix presides over small claims court downtown. Her husband Wendell Huang runs a party rental business, so the garage is full of folding chairs, bounce houses, and a podium. Renata's mother Bea, a retired high school basketball referee, lives in the converted den and still carries her whistle. The family rule, posted on the fridge: any dispute over five dollars or five minutes of yelling goes to Kitchen Court. The docket is a magnet board. The gavel is a wooden spoon. Three kids file most of the cases.

**Characters.**
- **Judge Renata Delacroix, 44.** Mother. Want: peace through process. Flaw: she cannot rule against her own kids without writing a twenty-page opinion. Line: "I've ruled on three thousand cases and never been appealed. Then I had children."
- **Bea Delacroix, 73.** Grandmother. Retired referee. Want: to be the one with the whistle. Flaw: she calls fouls on everything, including the dog. Line: "Traveling. You picked up the remote and moved. That's traveling."
- **Tobias Huang, 10.** Middle child. Want: to win one case. Flaw: he files too many. Line: "Your Honor, I'd like to enter into evidence the fact that she is, and I quote, 'looking at me.'"
- Also in the house: Wendell Huang, 45, father, who rents the courtroom furniture from himself. Marisol, 13, who represents herself and is good at it. Pip, 6, bailiff.

**Engine.** Sibling disputes become cases. Renata's real docket leaks home. Wendell's rental inventory turns the garage into any set the plot needs. Sample seeds:
1. The Case of the Last Waffle. Pip versus Tobias. Bea calls a jump ball.
2. Marisol sues for a later bedtime and hires Grandma as counsel.
3. Wendell rents a bounce house to the neighbor. It drifts onto the property line. Kitchen Court hears its first boundary dispute.
4. Renata gets a real case about a lemonade stand and has to recuse because Pip is a regular customer.
5. Tobias discovers "precedent" and re-litigates every ruling from last year.
6. Bea's whistle goes missing. The court cannot keep order. The dog is a suspect.
7. The kids draft a household constitution. Renata finds it better than the house rules and is troubled.
8. Family game night has no written rules. Everyone panics.
9. Pip takes the bailiff job seriously. Nobody can enter the kitchen without being announced.
10. Renata is asked to run for a higher court. The family holds a mock trial of her ambitions.

Engine yields 100: yes. Every sibling dispute since the beginning of time is a case. Renata's docket and Wendell's rentals add two more streams.

**Two-generation hook.** The parent gets the procedure jokes and a professional who cannot win at home. The kid gets to watch a kid file suit against a sibling and sometimes win.

**Tone and comedy source.** Character and situation in equal parts. The comedy is the gap between the seriousness of the format and the smallness of the dispute, and each character's relationship to rules: Renata needs them, Bea enforces them, Tobias games them, Pip loves them. The trials are short, formal, and warm. Nobody is humiliated on the stand. Verdicts are fair, and the losing kid gets a hug and dessert.

**Trailer hook (90 seconds, 4 beats).**
1. Close on the fridge docket. "Case 412: Who Ate It."
2. Court in session. Bea blows the whistle. Everyone freezes.
3. Marisol cross-examines Dad about the "no bounce houses indoors" rule while standing inside a bounce house.
4. Verdict. Pip bangs the wooden spoon. Renata: "Court is adjourned. Also, dinner."

Sample dialogue. Tobias: "Objection." Renata: "On what grounds?" Tobias: "He's winning."

**Production note.** Live action on one set: kitchen and dining room, with the garage as a second dressable space. AI-assisted production does well with the fixed kitchen, prop inserts, and the docket board. It struggles with a six-person table scene, so the trailer uses two-shots and a single wide.

**Risk.**
- IP distance: it might be mistaken for a courtroom show or a lawyer sitcom. It is not that. No judge show is set at a family table with a whistle-blowing referee grandmother and a bailiff who is six.
- Standards: nearest is 3.2, sarcasm from a child to a parent. Cross-examination of a parent must read as affection. Second nearest is 2.5, no humiliation. Losing a case is never humiliating.
- Biggest creative risk: format fatigue. Mitigation: vary case types, let some cases settle out of court, and let Renata's outside cases carry some weeks.

Standards section 2 self-check: pass.

## A3. Shift Change

**Logline.** With Mom on night shift at the hospital and Dad on morning radio, the three Abernathy kids live under three different governments every day, and Nana runs the biggest one.

**World.** A brick rowhouse in Pellston, a small city with one hospital and one AM station. Lorraine Abernathy is an emergency room nurse, seven at night to seven in the morning. Curtis Abernathy is the morning traffic reporter, on air from 4:30 to 9. Nana Velma, Lorraine's mother, moved in two years ago and runs the house from 3 in the afternoon to 8 in the morning under rules she invents. Each day has three handoffs. Each handoff loses information. The whiteboard in the hallway is the constitution, and it is always out of date.

**Characters.**
- **Nana Velma Prentiss, 68.** Grandmother. Want: to be indispensable. Flaw: she invents rules and backdates them. Line: "It's been a rule since before you were born. I just hadn't told you yet."
- **Curtis Abernathy, 44.** Father. Morning traffic reporter. Want: eight hours of sleep, once. Flaw: he reports on his own family like traffic. Line: "Folks, we've got a stalled eleven-year-old in the left lane of the kitchen. Expect delays."
- **Junie Abernathy, 11.** Middle child. Want: to exploit the gaps between governments. Flaw: a schemer who documents her own schemes in a binder. Line: "According to Dad it's bedtime, according to Nana it's 'quiet enjoyment,' and according to Mom, she doesn't know yet."
- Also in the house: Lorraine, 42, mother, who is awake for the wrong four hours. Desmond, 14, who has learned to be a diplomat. Otis, 7, who wants to know who is in charge.

**Engine.** Three regimes, three daily handoffs, and information loss at each one. The hospital and the radio station feed outside stories. Sample seeds:
1. Nana declares Tuesday a holiday. Nobody can prove it is not.
2. Curtis reads Junie's permission slip on air by mistake as a traffic bulletin.
3. Lorraine tries to stay awake for one school play and falls asleep standing. The kids build her a chair with a seatbelt.
4. Otis asks who is in charge and gets three answers. He appoints the dog.
5. The whiteboard is erased. The house enters a state of nature until Desmond writes a treaty.
6. Nana's oldest friend visits. Two grandmothers, two rulebooks.
7. Snow day. All three regimes overlap for the first time in a year. The parents meet at 2 in the afternoon like strangers.
8. Desmond gets a job at the hospital cafeteria and becomes his mother's coworker.
9. Curtis covers a night shift and Lorraine takes a day. The kids meet a father who is awake.
10. A blackout stops every clock. Nana declares it 1974.

Engine yields 100: yes. The handoff structure alone gives three scenes per episode. School, hospital, and radio supply plots.

**Two-generation hook.** The parent gets the exhaustion of shift-work parenting and the "who's on duty" chaos. The kid gets Junie working the gaps and Nana as the fun government.

**Tone and comedy source.** Character and world. The house is a country with three rulers who never meet. The comedy is what each ruler believes the others allow, and how the kids play the borders. Curtis's traffic voice, Nana's backdated law, and Junie's binder are the three recurring joke machines. Under it is one real thing: two parents who love each other and pass in a doorway once a day.

**Trailer hook (90 seconds, 4 beats).**
1. 6:58 in the morning. Lorraine walks in wearing scrubs as Curtis walks out with a thermos. They high-five without looking.
2. Nana rewriting the whiteboard with a ruler and a look of total authority.
3. Junie presenting a laminated chart of who allowed what, and when.
4. Snow day. The whole family asleep on one couch at 2 in the afternoon. The radio plays Curtis's own voice: "Roads are clear. Nobody's going anywhere."

Sample dialogue. Otis: "Who's in charge right now?" Nana: "Whoever's awake, baby."

**Production note.** Live action on one set: kitchen, hallway with whiteboard, living room. AI-assisted production does well with the fixed set at different light levels (dawn, dusk, 2 a.m.) and the whiteboard inserts. It struggles with physical comedy like the seatbelt chair, so the trailer keeps that as a reveal shot.

**Risk.**
- IP distance: it might be mistaken for a generic working-family sitcom. It is not that. The three-government structure, the traffic-report device, and Nana's backdated law belong to no existing show.
- Standards: nearest is 3.6, family shape. A three-adult household is never the joke. The schedule is the joke. Second nearest is 3.2.
- Biggest creative risk: the schedule bit gets old. Mitigation: the emotional spine is two parents missing each other, and the series arc moves them toward one shared hour.

Standards section 2 self-check: pass.

## A4. Lot One

**Logline.** The Farrow-Batiste family bought the model home of a subdivision that never got built, and now they live alone on a street with forty mailboxes and one house.

**World.** Meridian Ridge, outside a Sun Belt city. The developer paved three streets, planted forty mailboxes, built one fully furnished model home, and went bankrupt. The family got the house cheap. The furniture is staged: plastic fruit, books glued shut, a family portrait of strangers they have not taken down. The streets have names. The homeowners association has one member. The developer's new owner still sends prospective buyers on tours, through their living room, during dinner. Grandpa Lionel, a retired sign painter, has started renaming the streets.

**Characters.**
- **Lionel Batiste, 70.** Grandfather. Retired sign painter. Want: to make the empty street a real place. Flaw: he overbuilds. There is a gazebo on Lot 9 now. Line: "Every town starts with one stubborn man and a paint can."
- **Celeste Farrow-Batiste, 39.** Mother. Want: a real neighborhood so her kids have friends. Flaw: she runs the one-member homeowners association with terrifying zeal. Line: "I fined myself. I'm appealing. I denied it."
- **Dash Farrow-Batiste, 8.** Youngest child. Want: to be mayor. Flaw: he holds elections he always wins because the electorate is him. Line: "Lot 12 is the zoo. It doesn't have animals yet. It has potential."
- Also in the house: Andre Farrow-Batiste, 42, father, who works from home and takes calls from a different empty lot each day. Wren, 12, who wants one neighbor her age.

**Engine.** Forty empty lots are forty blank stages. Buyer tours bring guests through the house. Real neighbors arrive one at a time across a season. Sample seeds:
1. A buyer tour walks through during dinner. The family pretends to be part of the staging.
2. Wren throws a block party for a block with no people. A food truck shows up anyway. It is a success.
3. Dash's mayoral election gets a second candidate: the mail carrier.
4. Lionel paints a mural on the only house. The developer calls it "vandalism of the asset."
5. A second family moves into Lot 7. Celeste over-welcomes them into leaving.
6. Wren finds the one book that is not glued shut. It is the diary of the woman who staged the house.
7. Halloween on a street with one house. The family builds forty cardboard fronts and the whole town comes.
8. The streetlights turn on for the first time. The family has to decide whether they liked the dark.
9. Andre hosts an important video call from Lot 22 and a deer joins.
10. The developer proposes a cul-de-sac in their backyard.

Engine yields 100: yes. The lots are a blank canvas, the tours bring strangers weekly, and the slow arrival of neighbors is a multi-season arc.

**Two-generation hook.** The parent gets real estate and homeowners association jokes and the loneliness of moving somewhere for the kids. The kid gets an entire empty neighborhood as a kingdom, and Dash as its mayor.

**Tone and comedy source.** World first, then character. The world is a visual joke that never stops giving: a lit house on a dark grid. Character supplies the engine: Lionel builds, Celeste regulates, Dash governs, Wren longs. The comedy is warm and a little strange. The show believes a family can make a place out of nothing, and it lets that be sweet without saying so.

**Trailer hook (90 seconds, 4 beats).**
1. High shot at dusk. Three paved streets, forty mailboxes, one lit house.
2. A couple on a tour walks into the kitchen. Buyer: "Is the family included?" Celeste: "Depends on the offer."
3. Dash at a podium on an empty lot. "My fellow citizen."
4. Night. The whole family on the roof looking at the empty streets. Lionel: "Give it time. Towns take time."

Sample dialogue. Celeste: "The homeowners association says no." Andre: "You're the association." Celeste: "And I'm sorry."

**Production note.** Live action on one main set (the staged living room and kitchen) with exteriors. AI-assisted production is strong here: empty streets, mailboxes, dusk grids, and a single lit house are easy to generate consistently. Crowd scenes like the Halloween episode are the stretch.

**Risk.**
- IP distance: it might be mistaken for a "family moves to a strange town" premise, which is a broad category. It is not any one show. No existing show is set in a one-house subdivision with a one-member association and a self-elected eight-year-old mayor.
- Standards: nearest is 3.6, no family shape is a joke. Second nearest is 3.5, screen time is neutral. Andre's remote work is never a punchline about screens.
- Biggest creative risk: the world is too quiet. Mitigation: tours every episode, one arrival per few episodes, and a town that treats the street as a park.

Standards section 2 self-check: pass.

## A5. Revised Edition

**Logline.** When two families merge into one house, Grandpa Ray, a retired appliance-manual writer, writes the operating manual for the new family and updates it every week.

**World.** A big old house in Bellwood with more doors than rooms. Marcus Pell and his kids Nova and Emmett moved in with Yolanda Sarkis, her son Leo, and her father Ray. Ray spent forty years writing manuals for a dishwasher company. He has produced a spiral-bound document titled "The Pell-Sarkis Household, Revised Edition," with chapters, warnings, a troubleshooting section, and an appendix of incidents. Each week the family meets a situation the manual does not cover, and Ray issues a revision. The family shape is ordinary. The manual is the joke.

**Characters.**
- **Ray Sarkis, 74.** Grandfather. Retired technical writer. Want: order, and to be needed. Flaw: he writes warnings for things that have already happened. Line: "Section 4.2: Do not let Emmett near the blender. Added after the incident. Documented in Appendix B."
- **Nova Pell, 13.** Oldest child. Want: to find the loophole in every section. Flaw: she wins on technicalities and loses on people. Line: "The manual says 'bedtime.' It does not say 'sleep.'"
- **Yolanda Sarkis-Pell, 41.** Mother. Want: the merger to work so badly that she schedules bonding. Flaw: she cannot let a moment be unplanned. Line: "Tonight is Spontaneous Fun. Six to eight. Wear the shirts."
- Also in the house: Marcus Pell, 43, father, who reads the manual cover to cover and follows it. Leo Sarkis, 11, who has started his own competing manual. Emmett Pell, 9, subject of most warnings.

**Engine.** A new family produces a new first every week. The manual cannot keep up. Every episode ends with a revision read aloud. Sample seeds:
1. The first draft assumes the kids will read it. They do not. Ray records a video version and it is worse.
2. Who gets which bathroom. Ray produces a flowchart. Leo gets lost in it.
3. Nova and Leo notice that "step" is never defined and demand a glossary.
4. Emmett writes a manual for Ray. It is one page. It is accurate.
5. Marcus's mother visits with her own rules. The manuals conflict. Ray proposes a merger.
6. Troubleshooting: "House smells like fish. See page 41." Page 41 is blank.
7. Spontaneous Fun Night meets an actual spontaneous night when the power goes out. It is better. Yolanda takes notes.
8. Ray issues a recall. One rule made everything worse. He must find every copy.
9. Leo cites the warranty section and requests a replacement brother.
10. The hundredth revision. The family reads the whole thing aloud and realizes it is a diary.

Engine yields 100: yes. Every revision is an episode. A merged family supplies endless firsts, and the manual can cover any topic.

**Two-generation hook.** The parent gets technical-writing jokes, the terror of blending two households, and Yolanda's scheduled spontaneity. The kid gets Nova's loopholes, Emmett's counter-manual, and the recall episode.

**Tone and comedy source.** Character. Every joke comes from how each person relates to the document: Ray needs it, Marcus obeys it, Nova exploits it, Yolanda over-plans around it, Leo competes with it, Emmett is in it. The manual is a physical prop that people read aloud, argue over, and annotate. It is never a voice-over. The show is warm about the merger and honest that it is hard.

**Trailer hook (90 seconds, 4 beats).**
1. Ray at the dining table, reading from the spiral binder: "Warning. Contents may shift."
2. The bathroom flowchart taped to a door. Leo, in a towel, tracing it with a finger.
3. Yolanda handing out matching shirts that say "Spontaneous Fun."
4. Family dinner. Ray reads the newest revision. It is one line: "Section 9. We're doing fine."

Sample dialogue. Nova: "I found a loophole." Ray: "I know. I left it there. It's called being nice."

**Production note.** Live action on one set: dining room, kitchen, and the hallway of doors. AI-assisted production handles the fixed set and the manual page inserts well. Reading scenes are static and cheap. The stretch is six people around a table, so the trailer stays on pairs.

**Risk.**
- IP distance: it might be mistaken for the blended-family sitcom category. It is not any one show. The manual device and Ray's occupation belong to no existing series, and no character is an archetype lifted from a named show.
- Standards: nearest is 3.6, no family shape is a joke. The blended structure is treated as normal. Second nearest is 2.5, Leo's "replacement brother" request must resolve as affection.
- Biggest creative risk: the manual becomes a narration crutch. Mitigation: it is always read by a character in a scene, never over one.

Standards section 2 self-check: pass.
