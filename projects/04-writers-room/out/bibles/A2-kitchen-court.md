# KITCHEN COURT

Series bible. Anchor slot (Slot 1). Showrunner output for the A2 tournament winner (D-020).
Status: draft for the Orchestrator, the IP check, and the `standards` pass. Nothing here is canon until the Orchestrator writes it.

Placeholders: `[TOWN]` is the shared town name, left to the Orchestrator. `[BRAND]` is the block name, open at Fork D. The town name "Verano" from the tournament concept is withdrawn from this bible.

## 1. Title, logline, pitch

**Title.** Kitchen Court.

**Logline.** In a house where Mom is a real judge, every family fight goes to trial at the kitchen table, and when the kids sue their parents, a kid presides.

**The pitch a parent can repeat.** It's about a family where the mom is an actual small claims judge, so every fight in the house, a waffle, a bedtime, whose turn it is, goes to trial at the kitchen table. The grandma is a retired basketball referee, so she doesn't decide anything, she just blows a whistle and calls fouls. The six-year-old is the bailiff and she runs the toaster, and when your toast pops, your time is up. And the house rule is that when the kids sue a grown-up, a kid gets to be the judge. Every verdict ends with a snack and a hug, no exceptions. It's the only show where a first-grader can overrule a judge and the judge has to serve her a snack.

## 2. World

### The neighborhood

The Pinwheel. A subdivision on the edge of `[TOWN]` laid out in a ring road with cul-de-sacs off it, so the map looks like a pinwheel. Every street is a Court: Spoke Court, Hub Court, Vane Court. The Delacroix-Liang house is the last split-level at the dead end of Spoke Court. The kids know the joke. "We're Kitchen Court on Spoke Court." Nobody in the family finds it as funny as the mail carrier does.

The Pinwheel is a place where everyone's driveway faces everyone else's. Families vary in shape on the loop: a grandfather raising a grandson at the top of Hub Court, two moms with a tandem bike on Vane, a single dad who runs the block party. No family shape is a punchline, ever. The neighborhood matters to the engine because the Pinwheel learns, over Season 1, that there is a court at the end of Spoke, and starts bringing cases to the back door.

### The family's economics

Two incomes, neither big. Renata is a county small claims judge on a public salary. Wendell owns Liang Party Rental, a one-van business that is rich in June and broke in February. The house was bought before the kids. The living room is full of rental inventory half the year, so the family lives in the kitchen. That is not a design choice. It is a storage problem that became a home. Grandma Toni lives in the converted den a half-flight down, rent-free, and points that out only when losing.

Money is specific and never sad. The kids know what a deposit is because their father says the word forty times a week. Renata's job is stable and unglamorous. The show does not do money worry. It does money logistics, which is funnier and truer.

### What is specific and only theirs

- The court was founded by the kids, not the judge. Marisol, at seven, wrote the first rule on a napkin after a dispute about a swing. Renata was drafted, not appointed. She says so at least once a season.
- The judge does not judge grown-up cases. Rule Four on the fridge: a grown-up in the case means a kid on the Stool.
- The referee never rules. Toni's job is fouls, not verdicts. "I don't do right. I do fair."
- The gavel is a potato masher. The timer is the toaster. The bench is a step stool. The docket is the freezer door.
- No verdict is final until the losing side has picked the snack, the winning side has served it, and both have hugged across the table. Then, and only then, the whistle.

### The kitchen set, for the artist

One set. A split-level kitchen at the back of the house, an addition by a previous owner, bigger than the living room. Warm, worn, crowded, clean. Paper-bag tan walls, a green tile backsplash, a yellow ceiling light with a pull chain that nobody uses because the switch works.

**Layout, viewed from the camera side (the sink wall).**

- **Back wall, left:** the fridge. Off-white, older than Pip, magnets everywhere. The freezer door is the **Docket**: a strip of black tape at the top with the word DOCKET in Marisol's hand, and below it a column of case slips held by clothespin magnets. Left of the Docket, the **Rota**: three name magnets on a ring, MARISOL, ANSEL, PIP, with an arrow magnet pointing at whose turn it is to preside over a grown-up case. Right of the Docket, the **Fridge Rules**: five index cards under a pineapple magnet. Rule One: five dollars or five minutes goes to the table. Rule Two: one toast each. Rule Three: evidence fits on the table. Rule Four: grown-up in the case, kid on the Stool. Rule Five: snack, hug, whistle, done.
- **Back wall, center:** the back door, half glass, curtain on a rod, into the backyard. Used for neighbors and deliveries.
- **Back wall, right:** the door to the garage. Always slightly open. Through it, a slice of rental inventory: folding chairs stacked to the ceiling, a podium, a giant cardboard number. The inventory changes weekly and is the show's free set dressing. Items come through this door and into the story.
- **Right wall:** the split-level stairs. A half-flight up to the bedrooms, a half-flight down to Toni's den. A wooden half-wall with a rail. Characters appear from the waist up coming up, and from the head down going down. Toni's entrances start with the whistle on its lanyard rising into frame before her face does.
- **Left wall:** the range and counter. Above it, open shelves: mismatched mugs, the Binder (a fat white three-ring binder labeled HOUSE in marker, the rulebook and precedent record), and Roberta, a pothos plant that appears in Season 1 as a "trial pet."
- **Fourth wall (camera side):** the sink, the window over it, the dish rack. Seen only in the Sink Reverse, the frame used for the episode button.

**The table.** Center of the room, long axis left to right. An oval laminate table from Wendell's inventory with a rental tag still stapled under the edge. Nobody removes it. On the far side, facing camera, the **gallery bench**: a plain wooden bench where the parties sit, kids' side. On the near side, three mismatched chairs. At the left end, one chair with arms, Wendell's, called Facilities. At the right end, the head of the table, nothing, until court opens.

**The Stool (the "bench").** A red two-step kitchen step stool that lives by the fridge. When court opens, the bailiff drags it, scraping, to the head of the table. Whoever presides sits on it. Renata on the top step is a head taller than everyone and looks slightly ridiculous, which she knows. Pip on the top step is still not tall enough to see over the fruit bowl, so the fruit bowl is moved, which is her first ruling every time. The Stool at the table means court is in session. The Stool by the fridge means it's just a kitchen. That is the show's visual on-off switch.

**The Masher (the gavel substitute).** A heavy old wire potato masher with a chipped red wooden handle. It was Toni's mother's. It hangs on a nail by the range. The presiding judge taps it twice on the wooden cutting board to open, to rule, and to close. Two taps, never three. Three is a foul.

**The Cart (the bailiff's station).** A rolling metal kitchen cart with a four-slot chrome toaster on it. The bailiff wheels it to the judge's right hand. Each side of a case gets one slice. The Filer's slice goes down; the Filer speaks until it pops. Then the Filed's slice. Pop means stop. The toaster sits on 3. Pip alone may set it to 4, "for a big one," and everyone knows she never will for a bribe, because everyone has tried.

**The Wobble.** The witness seat. A wooden stool with one short leg that lives under the counter. Witnesses sit on it, put a hand on the Binder, and promise "to say what happened, not what it meant."

**Where the camera lives.** Along the sink wall, looking at the table, the fridge on frame left, the garage door on frame right, the stairs on the far right. Six fixed frames, listed in Section 11. The show is shot like a play at a table: wide when the family is a group, single when someone is on the Stool, two-shot across the table for the argument, inserts for the Docket, the Cart, and the Masher. The Sink Reverse is the only time the camera turns around, and it is used once per episode, for the last joke.

**Signature sounds.** The Stool scrape. The two taps. The toast pop. The whistle. All four are mixed at or below dialogue peak (Standards soft rule 8). Toni's whistle is an old brass pea whistle blown short and low, "the courtesy blow." The full blast is a once-a-season event and is mixed no louder than a shout.

## 3. Characters

Six regulars. Nobody else is a regular. Neighbors, litigants, and Wendell's teenage helpers recur as guests.

### Renata Delacroix, 44. Mother. "The Judge."

- **Job.** County small claims judge. She hears disputes between adults over deposits, fences, and cakes. The show never shows her courtroom. It comes home as stories and, sometimes, as people.
- **Want.** Peace through process. She believes a fair rule, fairly applied, ends any fight.
- **Flaw.** She cannot rule against her own child in one sentence. She writes an opinion. Eleven pages, once. Toni whistles her for delay of game every time. Secondary flaw: she keeps a private tally of who wins, and she is behind.
- **What she is funny about.** A professional who is never reversed downtown and is reversed nightly at home. She objects to herself. She cites the Binder against her own interest. When a kid presides over a case against her, she is the worst litigant the court has ever had, because she cannot stop coaching the judge.
- **How she speaks.** Precise, warm, complete sentences, slightly too many of them.
  1. "Downtown I've never been reversed. In this kitchen I'm 0 and 4 this week, and one of those was a first-grader."
  2. "I'm going to rule against you, and I want you to know it took eleven pages to get there, and I love you, and page six is where it turns."
  3. "You can't object to your own witness. You're the one who called her. She's your grandmother."
- **What the parent sees.** A competent adult who is good at her job and cannot win at home, and is fine with that. The parent's own week, made procedural.
- **What the kid sees.** A mom who has to follow the rules too. Proof that grown-ups lose sometimes and everybody still eats.

### Wendell Liang, 45. Father. "Facilities."

- **Job.** Owner, driver, and entire staff of Liang Party Rental. Every party in `[TOWN]` runs through his garage. Two teenage helpers in summer.
- **Want.** One dinner with no case. He has never had one.
- **Flaw.** He cannot say no to a rental request, so the house fills with things. He also cannot stand a dispute, so he proposes "split it" in every case and is overruled in every case.
- **What he is funny about.** The least legal person in a legal house. He supplies the furniture and has no standing. He accepts service on behalf of objects ("The house cannot be served. I'll take it."). He is secretly the court's biggest fan, because it is the only time everyone sits down at once.
- **How he speaks.** Easy, hopeful, slightly out of breath from carrying chairs.
  1. "Can we not split it? We could split it. I've got a knife right here, and I mean that in a kind way."
  2. "I'm not a party to this case. I'm a party rental. There's a difference and I've explained it to the fridge."
  3. "Forty chairs went out. Six stayed. The six are us. That was the plan. The plan was the six."
- **What the parent sees.** The partner who keeps the household moving while everyone else is arguing, and who wants credit only in the form of everyone sitting down.
- **What the kid sees.** The dad who brings home a chocolate fountain and gets in trouble for it. The fun one who is also, quietly, always there.

### Antoinette "Toni" Delacroix, 73. Grandmother. "Ref."

- **History.** Refereed high school basketball for forty years and one season of a semi-pro league nobody has heard of. Retired, mostly. Substitutes at the rec center. Lives in the den a half-flight down. Everyone in the house, including Renata, calls her Ref.
- **Want.** To be the one with the whistle. To be needed. She would never say the second part.
- **Flaw.** She calls fouls on everything, including the toaster. She refuses, on principle, ever to say who is right. "I don't do right. I do fair." This is a beautiful principle and it drives the family up the wall.
- **What she is funny about.** Household fouls with hand signals. Traveling (leaving the table with a case open). Hands (touching evidence). Holding (hugging to stall). Offside (talking before your toast is down). Delay of game (Renata's opinions). Three fouls and you lose your toast. She has a signal for "using a voice" that she invented in Episode 14 and defends as if it were ancient.
- **How she speaks.** Short. Flat. Certain. She announces calls like a scoreboard.
  1. "Traveling. You picked up your plate and moved. That's traveling. Sit."
  2. "I've watched two thousand games and I can tell you exactly one thing. Nobody who's mad is hungry, and nobody who's hungry is mad for long."
  3. "I'm not going to say who's right. I've never said who's right. I said who's out of bounds, which is you, and you know it, and I love you, sit down."
- **What the parent sees.** Their own parent, the one who lives with them, with a job to do in the house that is neither babysitter nor burden. Every hand signal is a laugh for an adult who once played anything.
- **What the kid sees.** The whistle. A grandmother who can end any fight with one sound and never takes a side, which means she is always on yours.

### Marisol Delacroix-Liang, 13. Eldest. "Counsel."

- **History.** Founded Kitchen Court at seven with a napkin. Wrote most of the Binder. Represents herself and, for a fee in snacks, her siblings.
- **Want.** To be taken seriously as an advocate. To win a big one. To have a friend sleep over.
- **Flaw.** She over-prepares. She turns a fight about a hoodie into a matter of record. She is thirteen, so she can argue a point flawlessly and then, in the same breath, ask if Nadia can sleep over.
- **What she is funny about.** Perfect procedure attached to a thirteen-year-old's priorities. She keeps notes. She has a folder for her brother. She is genuinely good, which is the joke, because she is good in a kitchen.
- **How she speaks.** Fast, formal, then suddenly not.
  1. "I represent my sister. She's six. She wants the top bunk and a lawyer, and she's got one."
  2. "Let the record show the hoodie was borrowed, not taken, and let the record also show it now has glitter on it, which is a Facilities problem, not a me problem."
  3. "Objection. Also, can Nadia sleep over? I'll withdraw the objection."
- **What the parent sees.** The kid who found her thing early and is a little too good at it. The parent laughs because Marisol's cross-examination sounds exactly like their own work email.
- **What the kid sees.** The big sister who can argue anyone into a corner and will do it for you, for a snack.

### Ansel Delacroix-Liang, 10. Middle. "The Filer."

- **History.** Files more cases than anyone. Has won two. Keeps count.
- **Want.** To win one outright, clean, on the merits, with no snack pity.
- **Flaw.** He files too much, and he is a terrible witness for himself, because he tells the truth compulsively, including the parts that lose him the case.
- **What he is funny about.** Courtroom drama energy on waffle-sized stakes. He enters things into evidence. He discovers words like "precedent" and "class action" and uses them on his sister. He is right once per episode, on his own logic, and nobody sees it coming, including him.
- **How he speaks.** Grave, dramatic, then honest to a fault.
  1. "I'd like to enter into evidence the fact that she is, and I quote, looking at me."
  2. "Objection." "To what?" "She's winning."
  3. "Okay, yes, I did it, but I'd like the record to show I did it after she did the thing, and also I'm sorry, and also I still think I win."
- **What the parent sees.** Their own middle kid, the one who needs a fair shot and a win. The parent laughs at the drama and roots for him every week.
- **What the kid sees.** The kid who sues his sister over a waffle and sometimes wins. Judge K's note, exactly. Ansel is the ten-year-old's way in.

### Philippa "Pip" Delacroix-Liang, 6. Youngest. "Bailiff."

- **History.** Appointed bailiff by Marisol at four, when she could not yet read the docket and announced it from memory, wrong. Now reads it. Wears a tea towel as a sash, folded and pinned, always the same towel, blue stripe.
- **Want.** For things to be in order. For everyone to be announced. She takes the job with total seriousness and no ambition.
- **Flaw.** Incorruptible, including about herself. She reports her own fouls. She announces her brother as "the Filed, my brother, who did it," and does not understand why that is a problem.
- **What she is funny about.** Literalism with authority. The Cart. The pop. "Time." When the Rota lands on her and she presides over a grown-up case, she rules with a simplicity that embarrasses everyone with a law degree. Her first act on the Stool is always to move the fruit bowl.
- **How she speaks.** Loud, exact, no contractions when announcing, all contractions when not.
  1. "Kitchen Court is open. Feet on the floor. Hands on the table. Dad, that's your chair, you know that."
  2. "Time." (The toast has popped. Ansel's mouth is still open. She does not blink.)
  3. "I'm the bailiff. I'm not on a side. I'm on the toaster."
- **What the parent sees.** Their kid at the age when rules are sacred and fair is a feeling. They see the tea towel and feel something.
- **What the kid sees.** A six-year-old with real power, who does not abuse it, and who gets to tell the grown-ups to sit. The six-year-old in the room sees herself and sits up.

### Distance note on characters

No character here is a version of a named character from any existing show. The tests applied: no character is defined by a catchphrase borrowed from anywhere; no character's job, age, and flaw combination matches any named sitcom regular the showrunner could identify; no child is a "wise-cracking kid" archetype from the nostalgia window (Pip is literal, not sarcastic; Ansel is dramatic, not snide; Marisol is formal, not cool). The referee grandmother who refuses to rule and the six-year-old bailiff who runs a toaster are new to this file.

## 4. Engine

### The case-of-the-week rules

Every episode has one A-case. The A-case is the spine. The Fridge Rules govern it.

1. **Rule One, the threshold.** Any dispute worth more than five dollars or five minutes of yelling goes to the table. Anything smaller is "kitchen business" and is settled by Wendell splitting it. The threshold is a running joke: the kids inflate stakes to reach it ("It's a waffle, but it's a five-dollar waffle to me").
2. **Rule Two, one toast each.** Each side gets one slice. Speak while it's down. Pop means stop. The toaster sits on 3. Only the bailiff may set it to 4.
3. **Rule Three, evidence fits on the table.** If it doesn't fit, it isn't evidence. A van, a bounce house, and a podium have each been argued to fit. None did.
4. **Rule Four, grown-up in the case, kid on the Stool.** If a parent or grandparent is a party, the judge is the next kid on the Rota. Renata may not preside over any case she is in, and neither may Toni, who would not anyway.
5. **Rule Five, snack, hug, whistle, done.** No verdict is final until the losing side has chosen the snack, the winning side has served it, and both have hugged across the table. Then Ref whistles. Then the Stool goes back to the fridge.

### The procedures as invented ritual

**Filing.** You write it on a slip. One line. You clip it under DOCKET on the freezer door. The bailiff reads the docket aloud before dinner, every night, whether there is a case or not. "The docket is empty" is a line Pip says with regret.

**Opening.** The bailiff wheels the Cart to the head of the table and drags the Stool over. Scrape. She says: "Kitchen Court is open. Feet on the floor. Hands on the table." The judge climbs the Stool, takes the Masher off its nail, and taps twice. Then the judge asks the opening question, which is the show's line: "Who's hungry and who's mad?" The mad one is the Filer. The other one is the Filed. (Not plaintiff. Not defendant. The family has never used those words and Marisol finds them "showy.")

**The toasts.** Filer's slice down. Filer talks. Pop. Filed's slice down. Filed talks. Pop. Interruptions are Offside. Ref calls it.

**Evidence and witnesses.** Evidence goes on the table. Witnesses take the Wobble, put a hand on the Binder, and promise "to say what happened, not what it meant." Ref calls Hands on anyone who touches evidence out of turn. Renata's flaw shows here: she asks the witness follow-ups like it's downtown, and Pip reminds her the witness is Dad.

**Fouls.** Ref calls them throughout with hand signals. Three fouls and you lose your toast, meaning you're done talking. Nobody has ever lost a case on fouls. Nobody has ever won one either. Fouls are about order, not outcome, which is the point of a referee.

**The verdict.** The judge taps the Masher twice and rules in one sentence. Renata cannot do this when a kid loses, so she reads from paper, and Ref calls delay of game. When a kid presides, the sentence is short and slightly devastating in its fairness. "Ansel gets the waffle. Pip gets the next waffle, and Ansel makes it."

**The Say.** The losing side gets one sentence, unanswered. Nobody may reply. It is the safety valve. Losers get to be heard after they lose. A 6-year-old watching learns that losing includes a turn to talk.

**Snack, hug, whistle, done.** The loser picks the snack from what's in the house. The winner serves it. This is the humiliation guard, the kid judge's rule, and the show's heart: the winner does the work, the loser gets the choice. Then the hug across the table. Then Ref whistles, short and low. Then Wendell says the words that close every case: "Court's closed. Dinner." The Stool scrapes back to the fridge.

### How cases escalate

Three ladders, used in that order.

1. **The Appeal to the Full Table.** A losing party may appeal once. All six vote by raising a fork. Ties go to Ref. Ref refuses to break ties. A tie therefore means "split it," which is the one outcome Wendell wants, so appeals are rare and everyone treats a tie as Wendell winning.
2. **The Binder.** A party may argue that a Fridge Rule is wrong. Changing a rule takes six forks, unanimous. This has happened twice. Both times Renata voted last and looked troubled, because the new rule was better than hers.
3. **The whistle.** Ref may end anything, any time, with the full blast. She has done it once. Nobody talks about it. It is held for the Season 1 finale.

Stakes escalate across the season without leaving the kitchen: waffle, bedtime, hoodie, whose calendar counts, whether the court takes outside cases, whether Mom takes a bigger court across the river. The mechanics never change. The size of the thing on the table does.

### The B-story engine, outside the court

Every episode has a B-story that does not go to trial. Four streams, one per episode, rotating.

- **Facilities.** Wendell's inventory of the week comes through the garage door and into the room: forty balloons, a photo booth, a giant cardboard number, a chocolate fountain. The town's parties pass through this kitchen. The object is set dressing, complication, and sometimes evidence.
- **Downtown.** Renata's small claims world leaks home as a story she can't tell, a litigant at the grocery store, a fruit basket she can't accept, a colleague at dinner. Never a courtroom. The thesis the show says out loud once a season: adults are kids with paperwork.
- **The Pinwheel.** Neighbors. Toni's rec-center games. The block party. The line at the back door once the court has a reputation. The world coming to the table.
- **The kids' week.** School reported at the table, a sleepover, a project, a trade. Marisol's friend Nadia, Ansel's best friend from the top of Hub Court, Pip's first-grade teacher.

The rule: the B-story must collide with the A-case at the verdict. The balloons are in the room when the ruling comes down. The litigant from the grocery store is on the Wobble. The collision is the act-three engine.

### Episode shape (22 minutes)

Cold open: a filing, or the middle of a case with no context. Act one: the docket read, the case opens, the B-story enters through a door. Act two: toasts, evidence, fouls, the B-story fills the room. Act three: the collision, the verdict, the Say, the snack, the hug, the whistle. Button: the Sink Reverse, one last joke from the other side of the room.

### Why the engine yields 100

Count the streams. Sibling disputes are infinite and cost nothing. Rule Four makes every parent-kid disagreement a case with a kid on the Stool, which is a second infinite stream with a different comic shape (a child being fair to a parent). Toni's fouls and refusals are a third. Wendell's inventory is a fourth that redresses the set every week for free. Renata's downtown is a fifth. The Pinwheel is a sixth that grows as the show grows. Holidays, weather, and structure episodes are a seventh. Section 8 lists 100 without strain and without a single one leaving the family's own logic. The tournament judges scored the engine 8 of 10. The showrunner's estimate after writing the seeds is that a room could write 200.

## 5. Tone and comedy

**Character first.** The joke is never "a courtroom in a kitchen." The joke is who these six people are when a rule meets a feeling. Renata's eleven pages. Wendell's "split it." Toni's refusal. Marisol's folder. Ansel's honesty. Pip's fruit bowl. If a line could be said by any character, cut it.

**Three rules the room follows.**

1. **Nobody is the fool for a full episode.** Everyone loses a case. Everyone is right once. Renata is competent downtown and funny at home. Wendell is overruled and indispensable. The 6-year-old is the bailiff and is never the joke.
2. **The rule is funnier than the fight.** Comedy comes from procedure applied to tiny stakes, with total sincerity. The toast pops mid-sentence. The van does not fit on the table. Never from anyone being cruel, and never from anyone being dumb.
3. **The kid is right once per episode, on the kid's own logic.** Not the parent's logic handed down. Pip moves the fruit bowl because she can't see, and it turns out nobody could see. Ansel's compulsive honesty loses him the case and wins him the Say.

**What the show never does.**

- Never shows a real courtroom, a robe, a gavel, or the word "bailiff" said by anyone but this family.
- Never a verdict without the snack and the hug, including the finale, including a two-parter.
- Never a case about a body, a weight, a diet, or a crush past a hand hold.
- Never a joke where a screen is the punchline. Devices exist in the house and are neutral.
- Never a joke about a family's shape, on Spoke Court or anywhere on the Pinwheel.
- Never a lost case that ends in a kid being shown up. The Say and the snack exist to make this structurally impossible.
- Never a real place, product, brand, or person. The town is `[TOWN]`. The rental company is Wendell's.

## 6. Two-generation design

Per scene type. The parent laugh and the kid laugh are independent, per the block bible's Two-Laugh test.

| Scene type | The parent laughs at | The kid laughs at |
|---|---|---|
| The Filing (fridge) | The slip's language ("Regarding: the thing she said at dinner"). The Rota arrow moving to a kid's name when a grown-up is sued. | Pip reading the docket aloud, wrong word and all. "The docket is empty" said like a weather report. |
| The Opening (Stool and Masher) | Renata, a real judge, climbing a step stool with dignity. | The scrape. The two taps. "Feet on the floor." Told to a dad. |
| The Toasts (Cart) | The parallel to every meeting they've sat in. A grown argument cut off by a toaster. | The pop. A mouth still open. "Time." |
| Evidence (table and Wobble) | Renata cross-examining her husband about a chair. "Say what happened, not what it meant" as a life rule. | Something enormous being argued to fit on the table. Ref calling Hands on Dad. |
| Fouls (Ref) | Toni's hand signals, which are real basketball signals repurposed for a household. Delay of game on an eleven-page opinion. | The whistle. Three fouls, no toast. A grandmother who never takes a side. |
| The Verdict (kid presiding) | A six-year-old's ruling that is fairer than anything they'd write. Renata coaching the judge and being overruled. | Pip moving the fruit bowl. Ansel freezing on the Stool. A kid telling Mom she lost. |
| The Say and the Snack | The loser picks, the winner serves. A parenting idea they'll steal. | The loser gets to choose. Nobody is in trouble. There is a snack. |
| The B-story door (garage) | Wendell's inventory as the town's social calendar. "Forty chairs went out. Six stayed." | A chocolate fountain in the kitchen. Forty balloons on the ceiling. |
| The Sink Reverse (button) | The last line, usually Renata or Toni, usually about the week. | The camera turned around. Someone at the sink doing dishes who lost. |

The one moment per episode a parent and a child can talk about after (soft rule 4): the Say. Every episode, someone loses and gets one sentence. The ritual kit's conversation prompt is always some version of "What would your one sentence have been?"

## 7. Season 1 arc (10 episodes)

**Season question.** Who decides? Renata's version: can I be fair to people I love? The kids' version: can a kid be the judge for real?

| Ep | Title | One line |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Last Waffle | Pip files against Ansel over a waffle with one bite out of it, Renata's first ruling against her own child runs eleven pages, and Ref invents delay of game. |
| 2 | Counsel | Marisol files for a later bedtime and hires Ref as her lawyer, and Ref, who has never argued a side in her life, hates every second and wins. |
| 3 | Six Chairs | Wendell rents forty chairs to the block party and forgets to keep six, the family eats standing up, Ansel files Family v. Facilities, and Rule Four puts Pip on the Stool for her first grown-up case. |

### Section 3, soft rules

1. **Slot 4 scares resolve.** Not applicable.
2. **Sarcasm from a child to a parent lands as affection.** This is the show's nearest risk and its central design problem. Five guards. First, the kids are not sarcastic by nature: Pip is literal, Ansel is dramatic and honest, Marisol is formal. Their comedy is sincerity applied to procedure. Second, Rule Four turns kid-versus-parent cases into a kid being fair to a parent, not a kid cross-examining one; the room writes the kid on the Stool as trying hard to be just. Third, Renata is never the fool; she loses on the merits and says so. Fourth, every kid win over a parent is followed by the parent losing gracefully and the hug, on screen, every time. Fifth, the room's line test: if a kid's line to a parent would sting said flat, it is rewritten until it only works with love in it. "Mom, you lost" is followed by "you can pick the snack."
3. **Body jokes at gross, never shame.** The Loud Chewing case (seed 20) is the closest. It is about a sound, not a body. No case is about anyone's size, skin, hair, or smell.
4. **One moment to talk about after.** The Say, every episode. The kit prompt: "What would your one sentence have been?"
5. **Screen time neutral.** Devices exist. The group chat is the parents' problem (seed 62). No line ever says the kids look at screens too much. No case is about a screen.
6. **Family shapes vary and none is a joke.** The Pinwheel is written with a grandfather-grandson household, a two-mom household, and a single-dad household as neighbors. They bring cases; their shape is never the case.
7. **Ordinary food, no diet talk.** Toast, waffles, cereal, cake, pie, a chocolate fountain, the last of the good coffee. Nobody is ever "being good" or "being bad" about food. The snack rule makes food a reward for everyone, including the loser.
8. **Sound levels.** The whistle, the pop, the two taps, and the Stool scrape are all mixed at or below dialogue peak. The full whistle in the finale is mixed at the level of a shout, not above.
9. **Flashing lights.** None. The photo booth (seed 53) flashes once per photo, spaced, never three per second.

## 10. IP distance statement

**What it could be mistaken for.** Two categories. The judge-parent sitcom: a family comedy whose parent is a judge and who brings the job home. The courtroom sketch: a mock trial at a domestic table, a shape that has appeared in sketch comedy for decades. The tournament judges scored A2 lowest on originality among the four winners (6.33) for exactly these reasons, and D-020 orders the bible to push the expression away from both.

**Five concrete choices that keep it distinct.**

1. **The judge does not judge the grown-up cases.** Rule Four puts a child on the Stool whenever a parent is a party. The professional judge is a litigant in her own kitchen at least once every few episodes, and a poor one. In a judge-parent sitcom, the judge judges. Here, the judge is judged, by a six-year-old, and serves the snack after.
2. **No courtroom vocabulary or objects.** No gavel, no robe, no bench, no plaintiff, no defendant, no "your honor," no "all rise." The court's objects are a potato masher, a step stool, a toaster on a cart, a freezer door, a wobbly stool, and a three-ring binder. The court's words are Filer, Filed, the Say, the Wobble, the Rota, and "Who's hungry and who's mad?" A courtroom sketch borrows a courtroom. This borrows a kitchen.
3. **The verdict is not the end.** The Say, the snack picked by the loser and served by the winner, the hug, the whistle, and "Court's closed. Dinner." The ritual after the ruling is longer than the ruling and is the show's identity. No courtroom sketch has a rule that the winner serves the loser.
4. **A referee, not a second judge.** Toni never says who is right. She calls fouls with hand signals and ends things with a whistle. A third adult who has authority over order and none over outcome is a new comic position, and it inverts the "grandparent who secretly runs the house" shape: Ref runs the clock and refuses to run anything else.
5. **The professional world never appears.** No downtown courtroom set, ever. Renata's job arrives as people at the back door, stories she can't tell, and a fruit basket she can't accept. The judge-parent sitcom cuts between court and home. This show has one room and the job comes to it.

Two more for the record: the court was founded by the children and the mother was drafted, and the family's second business (party rental) redresses the one set every week with the town's parties, which no home-courtroom premise has done.

**Requested check.** The IP check should confirm no existing series uses (a) a rotating child judge for parent cases, (b) a toaster as a speaking timer, (c) a referee grandparent with household fouls, or (d) the snack-hug adjournment. The title "Kitchen Court" should be cleared as a title in the same pass.
