## 1. Title, logline, pitch

**Title.** Recent History Society

**Logline.** A neighborhood historical society reenacts only what happened last week, in full costume, with total seriousness, and a grandfather and his granddaughter run it.

**The pitch a parent can repeat.** There is a club in a brick hall that treats last Tuesday like ancient history. The grocery store ran out of eggs, so twelve people in bonnets and hats stage the empty shelf as a fallen empire, with a narrator, a printed program, and a panel of historians who argue about which aisle it was. The founder is a retired bridge tender who thinks his errands are history. His ten-year-old granddaughter is the club's best actor and keeps getting cast as a fire hydrant. A mail carrier plays every horse. An archivist with a red card can stop the whole thing by saying "Point of History." Nothing they reenact is real. Everything they reenact is small. They take it as seriously as anything you have ever seen. The kids laugh at the falls. You laugh at the minutes.

## 2. World

### 2.1 The Society

The Recent History Society of Bell Hollow has twelve members, ages ten to seventy. It was founded "recently," which is what the banner says, because the founder refuses to set a date that would one day be older than a week. It meets every week in Foundry Hall. Its stated purpose, printed on every program: "So that last week is not forgotten."

The Society believes three things.
1. Anything that happened is history the moment it is over.
2. Small things that happened to us matter more than large things that happened to someone else.
3. If it is not reenacted, it did not fully happen.

Nobody outside the Society believes any of this. The Society does not mind. The neighborhood tolerates them the way a neighborhood tolerates a marching band: with affection, at a distance, and with a growing suspicion that they are right.

### 2.2 Bell Hollow

All four shows in the block share one town. This bible names only the neighborhood and leaves the town name to the slate ranking. Bell Hollow is the old foundry district: a canal, a lift bridge, a market, a branch library, a laundromat, a barbershop, a bakery with a flat roof, and one fire hydrant on the corner of Kettle Street that has now been played on stage more times than any hydrant in recorded history. The streets are short. Everyone shops at the same market. That is why every event has witnesses, and why the witnesses disagree.

### 2.3 Foundry Hall (the set)

An artist can draw this from the following.

- **The room.** A long brick room, the former pattern shop of a bell foundry. Tall arched windows down one long wall, wired glass, afternoon light. A wooden floor with a painted center line nobody remembers the purpose of. Exposed rafters. A cast bell hangs from a rafter above the lectern, with a pull rope. That is the foundry's last bell, and the Society rings it to open and close every meeting.
- **The chairs.** Forty folding chairs in six rows, metal, mismatched, three of them a different color. The Society has twelve members. The chairs are set for forty every week "in case."
- **The lectern.** Front left. Plywood, painted navy, with a hand-lettered card that reads COMMODORE. On it: the gavel (a small brass bell on a handle), the week's program, and a jar of pencils.
- **The banner.** Above the stage, cloth, hand-painted: RECENT HISTORY SOCIETY OF BELL HOLLOW. Beneath it, smaller, on a separate strip that has clearly been repainted many times: EST. RECENTLY.
- **The stage.** Front right. A low platform, two steps up, with a red curtain on a wire that sticks halfway. A painted backdrop of Bell Hollow: the canal, the bridge, the market. The backdrop has a slot cut in it so a head can appear through the market window. Props are cardboard and rolled wherever they are needed: a cart, a shelf, a bench, a bus door on a frame.
- **The costume rack.** Stage left, a long rolling clothes rack with hangers grouped under seven cardboard tabs: MON, TUE, WED, THU, FRI, SAT, SUN. Costumes are labeled by weekday, because the Society believes every day has a period look. Above the rack, a shelf of hats. Beneath it, a bin marked HORSE. Mrs. Tulliver guards the rack.
- **The Archive.** A four-drawer green steel filing cabinet with a brass plaque: THE ARCHIVE. It holds the evidence: receipts, ticket stubs, a bus transfer, a Polaroid of a puddle, a jar with a hailstone in the freezer compartment of a mini fridge beside it. The Archive is the highest authority in the Society. When the Commodore and the Accuracy Committee cannot agree, someone opens a drawer.
- **The panel table.** A folding table with a white cloth, three chairs behind it, three tent cards (HISTORIAN, HISTORIAN, COMMODORE), three microphones on stands that are not plugged into anything, and a pitcher of water nobody pours. This is where the reenactment is judged.
- **The sound booth.** A former foreman's office, up a short flight of stairs at the back, with a window that looks down on the hall. The light inside is always on. Member Twelve reads the narration from here. Member Twelve has never been seen at a meeting.
- **The back lot (the field).** Through a metal door at the back: a gravel yard behind the hall with a chalk line, a real fire hydrant, a picnic table, a flagpole with no flag, and a stack of pallets. Outdoor reenactments are staged here. The Society calls it "the field."
- **Field locations.** One per episode, brief, real: the market aisle, the library quiet room, the canal path, the four-way stop, the laundromat, the bridge. The Society goes to the actual site to settle a dispute or to place a plaque. The plaques are cardboard. They are removed by the following week, because by then the event is older than a week and no longer the Society's business.

### 2.4 The Bylaws

The bylaws are the engine. Every one of them exists to generate a specific kind of comedy. They are read aloud when violated, which is often.

**Article 1. The One-Week Rule.** An event qualifies for reenactment only if it happened more than one day ago and less than seven days ago. Anything older is "ancient history" and referred to the county museum, which has never replied. Anything from today is "current events" and out of bounds. Comedy: the clock is always running. An event that is six days old must be staged now or lost forever. An event that happens during a meeting cannot be staged until next week, and the Society must sit with that.

**Article 2. The No-Real-News Rule.** If it appeared on a screen, in a newspaper, or in any announcement from any office, it is not history. It is news. The Society does not do news. Only things a member witnessed, or things a member has an artifact of, qualify. Comedy: the smaller and more local an event, the more eligible it is. A sweater return outranks anything in the wider world, on principle. This bylaw is also the show's Standards guardrail: nothing real is ever reenacted. See section 5.

**Article 3. The Witness Rule.** Every event needs at least one member witness or one physical artifact. Witness testimony goes in the Archive. Artifacts go in the Archive. The Archive is never wrong, but it can be misfiled. Comedy: the Society is often reenacting something that one person saw and eleven people are imagining.

**Article 4. The Accuracy Committee.** Three seats, chaired by Dr. Imani Beale. The Committee may halt any reenactment by raising a red card and saying "Point of History." The Commodore must then rule. A ruling can be appealed to the Archive. The Archive is a filing cabinet. Comedy: a red card can stop a slow-motion fall halfway through. It often does.

**Article 5. The Objection Procedure.** Any member may object. The words are "Point of History." The objector states the error in one sentence. The Commodore rules "sustained" or "so noted." "So noted" means the error stays in, and it is written into the Corrections. Comedy: "so noted" is the Commodore's way of losing without losing.

**Article 6. The Casting Rule.** The Commodore casts every role. No member may play themselves, except by unanimous vote, which has never happened. Comedy: whenever a member's own errand is reenacted, somebody else plays them, in front of them, and they cannot object to the performance, only to the facts.

**Article 7. The Restaging Rule.** A sustained objection requires an immediate restaging, in costume, before the meeting continues. A restaging may correct only the objected detail. Everything else must stay exactly as it was, including any errors nobody objected to. Comedy: this is the escalation engine. Every restaging keeps the old mistakes and adds one correction that creates a new mistake. By the third restaging the reenactment is bigger, wronger, and more sincere than the event. See section 4.

**Article 8. The Costume Rule.** Costumes must be accurate to the weekday of the event. Weather must be matched. Comedy: nobody can explain what a Tuesday looks like, and Mrs. Tulliver can, at length.

**Article 9. Corrections.** Every meeting closes with Corrections, read into the record by the Accuracy Committee. Corrections are permanent. Corrections to Corrections are permitted and have happened. Comedy: the last word of every episode is a dry list of what the Society got wrong, and the Society is proud of it.

**Article 10. The Horse.** Not a bylaw. A tradition. Traditions are stronger than bylaws. Dale does all the horses.

**Article 11. Quorum.** Seven members. Twelve exist. Member Twelve is counted from the sound booth.

**Article 12. Adjournment.** The Commodore rings the bell and says the closing words: "Adjourned. Same time next week. Something will have happened by then." This is the show's closing line every episode. It is also the block's promise, said inside a story, without saying the block's name.

### 2.5 The opening ritual

Every meeting opens the same way. The Commodore rings the bell. He asks: "Who remembers?" The members answer: "We do." Then he reads the week's nominations. This is the show's call and response. An 11-year-old will do it at a lunch table within a month.

## 3. Characters

Five regulars. Each is original. None resembles a named character from any existing show.

### 3.1 Harlan Voss, 61. The Commodore.

- **Who he is.** Founder of the Society. Retired tender of the Kettle Street lift bridge, which is now automatic and which he still visits. He kept the bridge bell and the bridge cap. He gave himself the title Commodore because the bridge was technically a maritime post. He lives with his daughter's family and is Piper's grandfather.
- **Want.** For last week to be remembered. Underneath: for someone to ask him what he did today, and mean it.
- **Flaw.** He mistakes his own errands for history. He also cannot say "I was wrong." He can say "so noted."
- **What he is funny about.** Scale. He describes a sweater return the way a monument describes a battle. He is never sarcastic. He believes every word. His seriousness is the joke, and he never gets the joke, which is why the joke keeps working.
- **How he speaks.** In proclamations. Exact dates, exact times, the passive voice of a plaque.
  1. "On the fourteenth, at approximately four in the afternoon, I returned a sweater. The town held its breath."
  2. "Those who forget last week are doomed to repeat it."
  3. "Point of order is for meetings. Point of History is for history. Sit down, Dale."
- **What the parent sees.** A man who retired and built the one room where he still matters. The only person who believes in it completely is ten years old. He knows that. He has decided that is enough, and he is right.
- **What the kid sees.** The grandpa in the big hat who rings the bell, lets you fall down on purpose, and treats it like it counts.

### 3.2 Piper Voss, 10. Youngest member. Best actor.

- **Who she is.** Harlan's granddaughter. She joined the Society on the first day and has not missed a meeting. She learned the word "process" from a library book about acting and uses it as a weapon. She is the only member who rehearses.
- **Want.** A lead role. Any lead role. She has played the hydrant, the soup, a puddle, the ice, a pencil mark, and the beep on a voicemail. She has never played a person.
- **Flaw.** Too much feeling for inanimate objects. She cannot play a hydrant without giving it a backstory. The backstory is always tragic.
- **What she is funny about.** Total commitment. When she falls, she falls with grace and grief. When she stands still as a hydrant, she stands still with meaning. She takes notes on her own performance. She argues with the Accuracy Committee about the inner life of a shelf.
- **How she speaks.** Declarative. Professional. A working actor's vocabulary, deployed at ten.
  1. "I'm not a hydrant. I'm the hydrant that saw everything."
  2. "I need a minute. The soup had a family."
  3. "Grandpa, I can do Tuesday. I've been Tuesday before."
- **What the parent sees.** A kid who found the one place where adults take her completely seriously, and who has decided to earn it. Her sarcasm to Harlan is always in his own language, which is affection.
- **What the kid sees.** The kid who gets to fall down on purpose, in front of grown-ups, and is the best at it. The kid who is small and still the star.

### 3.3 Dr. Imani Beale, 38. Society historian. Chair of the Accuracy Committee.

- **Who she is.** An actual archivist at the county records office. She joined the Society to keep its records honest and has become the thing she hates most: the person who cares more than anyone in the room. She holds the red card.
- **Want.** Accuracy. Underneath: for accuracy to matter to someone besides her.
- **Flaw.** She footnotes conversation. She cannot let a wrong detail pass even when it does not matter, and she knows it does not matter, and knowing makes it worse. She has lost arguments to a ten-year-old about the emotional state of a fire hydrant, and she has records of each loss.
- **What she is funny about.** Receipts. She always has the receipt. She always has three. She is always right, and being right never once changes what the Society does.
- **How she speaks.** Precise. Dry. She cites. She pauses before "for the record."
  1. "That's not what happened. I have the receipt. I have three receipts."
  2. "I am not objecting to the horse. I am objecting to the horse's placement."
  3. "For the record, I was right. For the record, it did not help."
- **What the parent sees.** The person at every meeting they have ever attended who is right and alone. She is the parent's proxy in the room, and the show is kind to her.
- **What the kid sees.** The lady with the red card who can stop everything. Kids want that card.

### 3.4 Dale Pruitt, 45. Member. All the horses.

- **Who he is.** The neighborhood mail carrier. He walks every street in Bell Hollow every day, so he is the witness to most of its history and the reason the Society knows anything. He played a horse in the first reenactment because a horse was needed. He has played the horse ever since. There has not always been a horse in the event. There is always a horse in the reenactment.
- **Want.** To play something other than a horse.
- **Flaw.** He is too good at horses. When cast as anything else, he plays it as a horse. He has played a cart as a horse, a curb as a horse, and a leash as a horse. Every time, the Society tells him it was his best horse.
- **What he is funny about.** Hope. Every casting he raises his hand. Every casting he is a horse. He never complains. He asks one question, every week, and the question is now the most repeated line in the show.
- **How he speaks.** Few words. Sincere. Hopeful. He never raises his voice.
  1. "Is there a horse?"
  2. "I'd like to be considered for the cart."
  3. "I played the cart. Everyone said it was my best horse."
- **What the parent sees.** The colleague typecast at work and at the club, who is fine with it, and also not, and who shows up anyway. He is the show's gentlest running joke.
- **What the kid sees.** The horse. The horse is the funniest thing in every reenactment because it should not be there.

### 3.5 Mrs. Lorraine Tulliver, 70. Costumes.

- **Who she is.** She ran the Bell Hollow dry cleaner for forty years. When she closed it, she kept every garment that was never picked up. That is the costume stock. Every costume on the rack was left behind by someone in the neighborhood, and she knows whose. She sorts the rack by weekday because she believes each day has a look.
- **Want.** For everything to be as nice as it was last week.
- **Flaw.** Nostalgia for seven days ago. Every week she believes last week was a simpler time. Every week she is wrong, and every week she is more certain.
- **What she is funny about.** Her sincerity about Tuesday. Her refusal to let a Thursday coat appear in a Tuesday scene. Her long memory for who left which coat and why. She dresses a ten-year-old as a hydrant with the care of a wedding.
- **How she speaks.** Warm, slow, a little wistful about a period that ended on Sunday.
  1. "Tuesday was a simpler time."
  2. "You can't wear that. That's a Thursday coat."
  3. "I remember when this neighborhood had eggs."
- **What the parent sees.** The nostalgia joke turned gently on the block's own audience. The parent is watching a show built on the 1990s, and here is a woman who misses last Tuesday. The show never says this out loud. It does not need to.
- **What the kid sees.** The hat lady with the rack who can make you into anything.

### 3.6 Also in the Society (not regulars)

- **Member Twelve.** The narrator. A calm, straight-faced voice from the sound booth. Never seen. Reads the program aloud during every reenactment in the tone of a documentary about a war. Counted toward quorum. The Society does not know Member Twelve's name and has stopped asking. A production note on this voice is in section 11.
- **Six background members.** The Harmon twins, who vote together and always disagree with each other about it. Mr. Petrakis, who falls asleep and wakes up to object. Two teenagers, Wren and Oscar, who joined for community service hours and stayed. Old Nan Ferris, who was a witness to everything and remembers none of it. They are seen, they fill the stage, they rarely speak. They can be cast with local performers or, in tableaux, generated (section 11).

## 4. Engine and format

### 4.1 The 12-minute episode

The structure never changes. The changes inside it are the show.

| Segment | Minutes | What happens | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cold open: the Fall | 0:00 to 0:45 | The first image of every episode is a body or an object falling in slow motion, in costume, mid-reenactment, with no context. Member Twelve narrates one line. The bell rings. Cut to the hall. | Physical first. A 7-year-old laughs before a single bylaw is spoken. This answers the kid judge's note that the costume premise lands only after the falls start. The falls now start at zero. |
| The Call to Order | 0:45 to 2:00 | Bell. "Who remembers?" "We do." Harlan reads the nominations from the week. Members vote by raising their programs. The event of the week is chosen. Mrs. Tulliver names the weekday. Harlan casts. Dale asks his question. | The event and the casting are stated in dialogue. Nobody explains the Society. The show assumes you know, which is how a kid learns it. |
| The Reenactment | 2:00 to 5:30 | The event, staged. Printed program on screen for one second. Member Twelve narrates. Held tableaux. Slow motion. The horse. Piper's object. It goes wrong in character: Piper over-feels, Dale is a horse, Mrs. Tulliver stops it for a coat, Harlan expands his own role. | The reenactment is played entirely straight. No wink. The wrongness is the characters, never the camera. |
| The Panel | 5:30 to 8:00 | The Accuracy Committee at the table. Imani raises the red card. "Point of History." A dispute about a detail that does not matter. Harlan rules. A restaging (Article 7). A second objection. A second restaging. | The dispute is always about something small and specific: the aisle, the weekday, the number of eggs. The restagings escalate (4.2). |
| The Field Piece | 8:00 to 10:30 | The Society goes to the real site to settle the dispute or place the cardboard plaque. A brief location. Something is measured. A neighbor watches. The Society is dignified in public and the public is kind. | One location per episode. Short. The neighborhood is never the fool. The Society is not the fool either. Everyone is sincere. |
| Corrections | 10:30 to 12:00 | Back in the hall. Imani reads the Corrections into the record. Each is a joke and a fact. The last correction lands the episode's heart. Harlan rings the bell: "Adjourned. Same time next week. Something will have happened by then." | The last line is fixed. The second to last line is the episode's. |

Two or three segments per episode is the Wildcard rule. This structure reads as four short sketches with one spine: the Fall, the Reenactment, the Panel, the Field Piece, with Call to Order and Corrections as the wrap. The Wildcard tone band allows the show to break its own frame. This show breaks it by restaging: the same scene, again, wronger.

### 4.2 The one joke, and how it escalates by rule

The one joke: people treat the trivial as history. The Orchestrator's note (D-020) requires that this joke escalate by rule, not by mood. It does, on two clocks.

**Inside an episode: the Restaging Ladder (Article 7).**
1. The reenactment is staged once. It has three errors. Nobody objects to two of them.
2. Imani objects to one. Sustained. Restaging. The corrected detail forces a new error (the corrected aisle number puts the carts in front of the cardboard bakery, so now there is a bakery). The two unobjected errors remain, by rule.
3. A second objection, usually from the person the restaging displaced. Restaging. Now there is a bakery, a horse, and the original two errors, and the fall is bigger because the Society has "learned from the first two."
4. The Field Piece settles the fact and cannot settle the performance. The Society goes to the actual aisle and finds it is aisle five and a half, because the store renumbered on Wednesday.
5. Corrections record all of it. The errors are now permanent history.

The rule is the ladder. Every sustained objection makes the reenactment bigger and keeps everything that was already wrong. The joke cannot stay the same size, because the bylaws will not let it.

**Across a season: the Scale Ladder.** The writers' room orders the season by what kind of event is reenacted, so the premise changes shape every two episodes.

| Rung | Kind of event | What it does to the joke | Season 1 episodes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ordinary events | Establishes the liturgy. The egg shortage, the four-way stop. | 1, 2 |
| 2 | Smaller events | Shrinks the subject, grows the seriousness. A sweater. A tooth. The Self Rule forces cross-casting. | 3, 4 |
| 3 | Disputed events | Two witnesses, two reenactments, one stage. The panel becomes the story. | 5 |
| 4 | Events nobody saw | Only an artifact. The Society reconstructs a hailstone's fall from a jar. Pure invention performed as record. | 6 |
| 5 | The Society itself | Recursion. They reenact their own last meeting, and the no-horse week. The bylaws point inward. | 7, 8 |
| 6 | An event too big | The neighborhood asks for the founding of Bell Hollow. Too old. They refuse, and reenact the planning meeting instead. | 9 |
| 7 | An event during the reenactment | The lights go out mid-scene. It happened today. By Article 1 they cannot touch it for a week. The show's rule finally costs them something. | 10 |

Season 2 climbs again from a new base: events with two locations, events in weather, events that the Society caused, an event that is a Correction, an event that is a week with no meeting. The rungs are reusable. The rule is: the season never stays on one rung for more than two episodes.

### 4.3 Why the engine yields 100 episodes

The engine has four independent inputs. They multiply.

1. **The event.** There is always a last week. Every week supplies a small event, and the event never has to be good, because the Society makes it good. This input is unlimited. Section 8 lists 100 without strain and stops only because the brief said 100.
2. **The casting.** Article 6 (no member plays themselves) and Article 10 (Dale does the horses) mean the same event can be staged three different ways depending on who is cast as whom. Piper as Harlan is a different episode from Dale as Harlan.
3. **The dispute.** The Accuracy Committee objects to one detail. Which detail is a choice: the day, the place, the count, the weather, the order, the participants, the motive, the inner life of a shelf. Each generates a different restaging ladder.
4. **The field.** One real location per episode. Bell Hollow has dozens of small sites, and the Society treats each like a battlefield.

Any event times any casting times any dispute times any field is more than a hundred. The constraint is not supply. The constraint is the Scale Ladder, which keeps the show from staging the same size of joke twice in a row.

### 4.4 The heart

The show's spine is Harlan and Piper. He needs last week to matter. She needs a lead role. Each episode gives one of them something, never both, until the finale. They take each other seriously, always. Piper never rolls her eyes at Harlan. Harlan never talks down to Piper. Their scenes are the moment the parent and the kid on the couch look at each other.

## 5. Tone and comedy

**Mock-serious, played straight.** The Society is never winking. The camera is never winking. Nobody in the show knows it is a comedy. The audience is the only one laughing, and the audience is laughing with them, because the Society is doing something silly with complete dignity, and dignity is the joke and also the point.

**Three rules the room follows.**
1. **Everyone is sincere.** No character is ever in on it. Harlan believes. Imani believes in accuracy. Piper believes in the hydrant. If a line only works because a character is being ironic, cut it.
2. **The event is small. The staging is not.** The gap between the size of the event and the size of the effort is the only source of comedy the show needs. Never make the event bigger to get a laugh. Make the effort bigger.
3. **The bylaws decide, not the writer.** When the room does not know what happens next, it reads the bylaws. A sustained objection means a restaging. A member's own errand means cross-casting. A missing witness means the Archive. If the bylaws produce the beat, it will feel inevitable, and inevitable is funny.

**What the show never does.**
- **No real news.** Article 2 is a bylaw and a room rule. Every event is invented and local. Nothing on screen has happened anywhere.
- **No real people.** No public figure, no real business, no real brand, no real place name outside the invented neighborhood.
- **No politics.** The Society has bylaws, votes, and a Commodore. None of it is government. The Society never reenacts anything from any office, council, election, or public body, and no character holds any public role. The procedure jokes are about the Society's own rules, and the rules are absurd on purpose. Any line that could be read as a comment on a real institution is cut.
- **No religion.** No holiday with religious content is reenacted. No place of worship appears. The Society's only sacred object is a filing cabinet.
- **No mockumentary devices.** No talking-head interviews, no handheld camera, no character addressing the lens, no on-screen text captions naming the interviewee. The show is scripted, staged, and lit like a small play.
- **No injury.** Falls are pratfalls, in slow motion, in tableau, with dignity. Nobody bleeds. Nobody limps. The soup gets up.

## 6. Two-generation design

**What the 7-year-old laughs at.**
- The Fall at the top of every episode. A pyramid of soup cans going over in slow motion, with a kid inside it.
- The horse. There is always a horse. There should never be a horse.
- The hydrant, and the girl who plays it with a face that says she has seen things.
- The bell. Kids love a bell. The bell is rung with full ceremony for things like a sock.
- Costumes: bonnets, a tricorn cap, a cardboard cart, a horse head in a grocery store.
- Mrs. Tulliver stopping a scene to swap a coat.
- Repetition with variation: the same scene restaged three times, each time bigger, is a joke a 7-year-old can predict, and predicting it is the pleasure.

**What the parent laughs at.**
- Procedure. "Point of History." "Sustained." "So noted." The Corrections.
- Gravity about nothing. A sweater return with the cadence of a monument. "The town held its breath."
- Imani, who is right every week and whose rightness changes nothing.
- The Restaging Rule, which keeps every error by design. The parent has sat in this meeting.
- Mrs. Tulliver's nostalgia for Tuesday, which is the block's own nostalgia held up to a small mirror.
- The line the parent judge flagged: "That's every week, Harlan. That's how weeks work."
- Harlan and Piper taking each other seriously. This is the one the parent does not laugh at. It is the moment they can talk about after (Standards soft rule 4).

**What the 11-year-old repeats at school.**
- "Point of History." (Used to interrupt a friend who got a detail wrong.)
- "Who remembers?" "We do."
- "Is there a horse?"
- "I'm the hydrant that saw everything."
- "So noted."
- The slow-motion fall, performed in a hallway, with narration.

## 7. Season 1 arc

**The season question.** Does last week matter if the only people who remember it are the twelve of you?

**How the season answers it.** Yes, and the proof is not the reenactment. The proof is the room.

| Ep | Title | One line |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Great Egg Shortage of Tuesday | The market ran out of eggs for four hours, two carts met in aisle five, and the Society stages it as a fallen empire while Piper, as the soup, gets her first standing ovation and no lead. |
| 2 | The Battle of the Four-Way Stop | Four bicycles arrived at once and nobody yielded for eleven minutes; the field piece at the real corner ends with a cardboard plaque and Dale as all four horses. |
| 3 | The Sweater | Harlan returned a sweater without a receipt; the Self Rule means Piper plays Harlan, and she plays him better than he does, which he sustains. |
| 4 | The Tooth | Piper lost a tooth in the library quiet room; Dale must play Piper, asks whether the tooth can be a horse, and is told "so noted." |
| 5 | The Two Bake Sales | Two witnesses disagree about which table sold out first; two reenactments run on one stage at once, and Imani's red card is raised so often it is retired for the week. |
| 6 | The Hailstone | A hailstone the size of a plum landed in the back lot and nobody saw it; the Society reconstructs the fall from the jar in the Archive, and Piper finally plays weather. |

## 10. IP distance statement

**Categories it could be mistaken for.**
1. Mockumentary comedies about small clubs, offices, or civic groups, shot with talking heads and handheld cameras.
2. Reenactment sketch: comedians restaging events with deliberate cheapness.
3. Historical society or local museum sitcoms.
4. Grandparent and grandchild two-handers.

**Five concrete choices that keep it distinct.**
1. **No mockumentary device.** No talking heads, no handheld, no glance at the lens, no caption cards naming a speaker. The show is staged and lit like a small play with a fixed proscenium. This alone removes it from the first category.
2. **The One-Week Rule.** The Society reenacts only the last seven days. No existing show or sketch has this rule. It is the show's most original sentence (producer judge) and every bylaw hangs from it.
3. **The Restaging Rule and the Corrections.** The reenactment is restaged inside the episode with accumulated errors, and the episode ends with a read list of what was wrong. This is a structure, not a bit, and it belongs to no other show.
4. **Member Twelve and the Archive.** An unseen narrator who is counted toward quorum, and a filing cabinet that outranks the founder. Two invented objects that could belong to no other show.
5. **A ten-year-old lead who only plays objects.** Piper's want (a lead role) and her flaw (too much feeling for a hydrant) are specific and original. No named character in any existing show is a child reenactor typecast as a fire hydrant by her grandfather.

Additional distance: the setting is an invented neighborhood with an invented foundry. The costume rack is sorted by weekday. Nothing real is reenacted. The horse tradition is original. The closing line is original. The IP check agent should confirm against the four categories above.

## 11. Production note

**What AI-assisted production can carry (CG-03, with disclosure).**
- **Costume stills.** Each member in each weekday costume, on the hall stage, against the painted backdrop. A still library of the rack.
- **Program pages.** The printed program for each event: title, cast, "a Tuesday," a woodcut-style illustration of a shopping cart.
