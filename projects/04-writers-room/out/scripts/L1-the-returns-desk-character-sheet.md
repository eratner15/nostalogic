# The Returns Desk. Character and set sheet.

Slot 4, Lights Out. Staff-writer output against `projects/04-writers-room/out/bibles/L1-the-returns-desk.md` sections 2, 3, and 11. Written for two readers: a casting director, and whoever writes prompts for an image generation model during concept art.

Rules for anyone using this sheet.

1. Every person here is original. No description points at a real actor, a real person, or a character from any existing show. If a generated image resembles a real person, discard it.
2. Any generated image that carries a face or a performance is logged at counsel gate CG-03 and disclosed before public use. The bible's recommendation stands: generated frames for the trailer carry objects, aisles, the window, the shelf, and insert stills with no people in them.
3. Nieves is a child. The bible forbids generating any real child's likeness for any reason. Use her entry for casting (CG-04) and for the art department. If concept art needs her, draw her, or frame her stool and ledger with the seat empty. Do not generate a photoreal child.
4. The "one prop" is the object the character is holding when a viewer pictures them. It appears in every key art frame that character is in.
5. Frame names follow the bible. Setup A is the desk two-shot. Setup B is the item close-up. Setup C is the reverse over Marguerite's shoulder. The insert is the customer's story, shot away from the store. The swinging door is Dolores's frame.

## 1. Marguerite Salcedo, 63. The desk.

**Age and build.** 63. Medium height. Solid through the shoulders from thirty-one years of lifting other people's returns onto a counter. Not heavy, not thin. Built to stay seated.

**Face.** Wide, with a square jaw and heavy-lidded dark brown eyes that take their time. Deep lines beside the mouth that she rarely uses. No makeup. Reading glasses with tortoiseshell frames on a beaded chain, worn low on the nose, looked over more than through.

**Hair.** Thick, steel gray with darker gray underneath, cut blunt at the jaw. One side tucked behind an ear. A yellow pencil lives behind that ear.

**Skin.** Warm olive brown. Weathered on the hands only. Short nails. A thin plain silver band on the right hand, not the left.

**Constant costume.** A maroon store vest with a stitched HARDISON'S patch on the chest. The patch is older than the vest and has been moved from vest to vest. Under it, a plain oatmeal long-sleeve shirt, sleeves pushed to the forearm. A name tag that says MARGUERITE in her own hand on a strip of masking tape stuck over a printed tag that once said something else. A gray cardigan hangs on the back of her stool. She has never been seen wearing it.

**The one prop.** The hand-cut rubber stamp that reads STORY HEARD. Red ink. Wooden handle worn pale where her thumb sits.

**Posture.** Seated. Elbows on the brass edge strip of the counter, weight forward, hands folded or holding a pen. She never leans back. She never stands up for a story. She stands up for a stepladder.

**Frames.**
- Setup A: frame right, seated, lit warm from the gooseneck lamp at her left, the shelf soft behind her. Her face is the calm half of the frame.
- Setup B: her hands only. One hand flat on the counter beside the item. The other holds the tag, the pen, or the stamp. The silver band catches the lamp.
- Setup C: her left shoulder and the gray hair in the lower right corner of frame. The pencil behind the ear is the only detail in focus.
- The swinging door: she is never in it. She turns her head toward it when Dolores speaks and nothing else moves.

**Casting note.** Low, slow, flat voice. Full sentences. She is funny because she is never in a hurry and never surprised. Cast for stillness.

## 2. Nieves Ruiz, 12. The ledger.

**Age and build.** 12. Small for twelve. Straight up and down, all elbows and knees, in the last year before she grows.

**Face.** Round, with dark, level eyebrows that do most of her acting. Brown eyes. A small gap between the front teeth. She frowns when she is interested, which is often.

**Hair.** Black. One long braid down the back, neat at the start of the shift and unraveling by the button. By the end of every episode there is a pencil through it. This is a family trait.

**Skin.** Light brown. A scab on one knuckle from something at school she has not explained.

**Constant costume.** A mustard yellow hooded sweatshirt two sizes too big, sleeves pushed up past the elbow and sliding back down all night. A striped long-sleeve shirt under it. Jeans. Sneakers, one lace double-knotted, the other not. A lanyard around her neck with a laminated card that reads STAFF (KID) in her mother's handwriting. A small clip-on reading light on the ledger.

**The one prop.** The green cloth ledger. Columns in her hand: ITEM, CLAIM, CLUE, CAUSE. A clipboard travels with it.

**Posture.** Perched on a tall stool at the far end of the counter, one foot hooked on the rung, the other swinging. When a story gets good she comes up off the stool onto both forearms and leans across the counter. She never sits still and never leaves the desk.

**Frames.**
- Setup C: far end of the counter, frame right, ledger open, lit by lamp spill and her own clip light. Marguerite's shoulder is always in the same frame. This is her home frame.
- Setup A: she enters at the far left edge of frame when she leans in, half a face and a pen. She is never framed alone here.
- Setup B: never alone. Her hand may enter the top of frame with a pen, pointing at a clue on the item, with Marguerite's hand already in the frame.
- The swinging door: she is in the foreground when it opens. Dolores is behind her. Nieves does not turn around. She closes the ledger.
- Rule for every frame: Marguerite in it, or the swinging door with the porthole shadow in it. Never the mouth of Aisle 12 behind her alone.

**Casting note.** Fast, precise, speaks in threes. She should sound like a clerk asking questions and a scientist stating answers. Cast a child who can be still and serious and let the adults be funny around her. CG-04 applies.

## 3. Teddy Vance, 16. The stock boy.

**Age and build.** 16. Tall and thin. Has not grown into his hands or his feet. Shoulders up around his ears as a resting position.

**Face.** Long and pale, with freckles across the nose, ears that stick out and go red, and one eyebrow that is always slightly higher than the other. A wide, easy smile that arrives about two seconds after every scare.

**Hair.** Reddish brown, curly, flattened on one side by a knit beanie he takes off inside and stuffs in his back pocket.

**Skin.** Pale and freckled. The ears are the tell.

**Constant costume.** The same maroon store vest as Marguerite, too short in the body, over a plain gray T-shirt. Work pants with a tape measure clipped to the belt that he has never once used. High-top sneakers. A headlamp worn around his neck, never on his head, because on the head "it makes shadows."

**The one prop.** A push broom. He holds it upright in front of him like a railing and walks behind it.

**Posture.** Standing like he is about to leave. Weight on the back foot. When he walks into Aisle 12 he walks in forward. When he comes out he comes out backward.

**Frames.**
- Setup C: crossing behind the customer, left to right, on his way to Aisle 12, broom first. Later, the same crossing, right to left, faster.
- The ice machine frame: Setup C pulled wide enough to include the ice machine at frame left. Teddy beside it, both hands on the broom, looking straight at it, waiting. This is the show's cheapest laugh and it is his.
- Setup A: rarely. When he is a customer (Episode 3, Episode 5) he stands frame left and holds the item like a customer, and the vest makes it funnier.
- The swinging door: never. He does not go to the dock at night. Nobody has asked him why.

**Casting note.** Reassuring himself out loud, in lists. Brave in the mouth, not in the body. He must be likable at his most scared and the first to laugh after. Never cast for mockery.

## 4. Ambrose Quayle, 74. The regular.

**Age and build.** 74. Tall, narrow, stooped from the neck so that his head arrives before the rest of him. Long, careful hands.

**Face.** Long and fine-boned. A white mustache trimmed with precision. Hooded pale gray eyes. A thin nose. A face that reports the most ordinary object with the calm of a man reporting a comet.

**Hair.** White, thin, combed wet, with a visible comb line. A flat tweed cap, always removed at the door and held in the left hand for the whole visit.

**Skin.** Brown, papery, with the rough hands of a man who kept a garden and now keeps a garage.

**Constant costume.** A tweed sport coat over a buttoned cardigan over a shirt buttoned to the collar. No tie. Pressed trousers. Polished brown shoes. A folded plastic bag from a shop that closed years ago, in the coat pocket, in case he needs to carry something home.

**The one prop.** Whatever he is returning tonight, held carefully, plus the receipt for something else. The flashlight is the key art version: held upright like a candle.

**Posture.** Perfectly straight at the counter, item set down in front of him, hands folded on top of it, patient as furniture. He does not lean. He does not look at the shelf, where at least three of his things live.

**Frames.**
- Setup A: frame left, standing, cap in hand, item on the counter. The politest silhouette on the show.
- Setup B: his hands folded on the item, and the wrong receipt smoothed flat beside it.
- Setup C: entering through the front door under the spring bell, the streetlight behind him, taking the spot beside whatever customer is already there as if it were reserved.
- The insert: his own garage, lit by his own flashlight. The tidiest garage in the Flats. Every tool on a hook. It is always his own tidying.

**Casting note.** Formal, courteous, unhurried, and never the punchline. His returns are. He begins in the middle of every story. The audience must want him to stay.

## 5. Dolores Ruiz, 41. The dock. Supervising adult.

**Age and build.** 41. Medium height, strong through the arms and back. Moves fast and stops fast.

**Face.** Nieves's face with twenty-nine more years on it. The same level eyebrows. Tired eyes that are still quick. A mouth that is already halfway into the next thing.

**Hair.** Black, pulled back hard into a bun. A pencil through the bun. The family trait started here.

**Skin.** Light brown. A smear of box dust on one cheekbone that she does not know about.

**Constant costume.** A canvas work apron with deep pockets over a flannel shirt, sleeves rolled to the elbow. One work glove on, one in the apron pocket. A dock headset around her neck. Steel-toe boots. A roll of packing tape worn on the wrist like a bracelet.

**The one prop.** The swinging door itself. She holds it open with a shoulder and never quite comes through it.

**Posture.** In a doorway. Weight on the door. One hand free to point. She is in charge of the room from its edge.

**Frames.**
- The porthole: a shadow crossing behind the round window, several times an episode. Never a face pressed to the glass.
- The swinging door: the door opens toward camera and she fills the gap, apron, glove, headset, pencil. She says her line. Nieves closes the ledger. This frame ends every episode.
- Setup A: once a season, at most. When she comes all the way to the counter, something has happened.
- The intercom: a voice from a small gray speaker box on the wall beside the door, with a red indicator that glows steadily while she talks. It does not blink.

**Casting note.** Competent, tired, funny, and never once unsure where her daughter is. Every line she has is a working parent's line.

## 6. Guest for the pilot and trailer: Desmond Nunes, 41.

The customer of Episode 101. Not a regular. Included so the trailer and the cold open cast the same man.

Medium build, average height, a soft face that has been up too long. Dark hair that has made its own decisions. A raincoat over a pajama top, jeans pulled on in a hurry, and slip-on shoes. Carries a shoebox in both hands like a pie. His one prop is the coin battery he sets down as evidence. He stands frame left in Setup A and holds the counter with both hands. He is reasonable, thorough, and scared in the way anyone would be. He is never the fool. He leaves lighter.

## 7. The returns desk set

Written as one wide stage for the artist, then as a prompt.

**Stage.** One long room. The returns desk at center right. The front door at far left. Aisle 12 upstage, running away from camera into darkness. The back half of the overheads are off.

**The counter.** L-shaped, waist high. Yellowed laminate with a chipped front edge. A brass edge strip worn silver where elbows rest. The short leg faces the door. The long leg faces camera. A gooseneck lamp on the long leg is the key light and the warmest light in the room.

**On the counter.** A domed brass service bell on the short leg, at the customer's left hand, with no tag on it. A spindle spike of receipts. A tape dispenser chained to the counter. A tray with two rubber stamps: RETURNED, and the hand-cut STORY HEARD. A cardboard box of manila tags on string. A cup of pens, every one from a different business. Under the counter, in the drawer, the green ledger.

**The one window.** Beside the front door. Plate glass with HARDISON'S HARDWARE in reversed gold leaf. Outside, one streetlight, the rail spur, the cannery's sodium glow far off, and an occasional slow headlight sweep across the shelf. No face is ever at this window. This is the rule of the room and it is a rule for the artist as well.

**The front door.** Glass, with a spring bell on a bracket. Every customer enters through it. Every exit rings it.

**The wall clock.** A round shop clock above the shelf. It was a return. Render it reading a different time in every frame. No one on the show ever says the time.

**Palette.** Warm amber at the desk from the lamp. Cool green-white fluorescent in the front half of the store. Blue-black at the far end of Aisle 12. Sodium orange through the window. The maroon of the vests. The manila of the tags.

**Prompt for the set plate (no people).** "Interior of a small neighborhood hardware store at night. An L-shaped returns counter of yellowed laminate with a worn brass edge, lit by a single warm gooseneck lamp. Behind the counter, floor-to-ceiling industrial metal shelving on a pegboard wall, holding ordinary hardware items, each with a manila paper tag on string. A glass front door with a spring bell. A plate-glass window with reversed gold-leaf lettering, one streetlight outside. A chest-style commercial ice machine with a hand-lettered ICE sign against the wall between the counter and the door. A swinging door with a round porthole window and a STAFF sign at the end of the counter. Aisles of paint cans running back into darkness. Fluorescent lights on in the front half only. Warm, still, quiet. No people. No faces in the window."

## 8. The Shelf of Returned Things

Floor to ceiling, industrial metal shelving on a pegboard wall directly behind Marguerite. Every item that came back with a story sits on it with a manila tag in Marguerite's handwriting: the item on the top line, the cause on the line under it. The tags are the only text on the shelf. It grows one item per episode and never loses one.

At the top left corner, the oldest tag. Brown at the edges, string gone gray, handwriting younger. Blank. It is on screen from Episode 1 and nobody points at it until Episode 5.

**What sits there at the start of Season 1.** Ordinary things only: a wind-up kitchen timer, a rain gauge, a push broom, a bath plug, a rake, a cup hook, a length of rope, a plunger, a set of house numbers, a doormat rolled and tied. Nothing antique. Nothing with a face. Nothing that was ever a toy. No doll, no mask, no mirror, no book. This is a warehouse shelf with paper tags and it must look like one.

**Light.** Lamp spill on the lower shelves, warm. Fluorescent on the middle. The top shelf catches the streetlight through the window and nothing else. When the lamp goes off at the end of the episode, the tags are the last things visible.

**Prompt for the shelf plate (no people).** "Industrial metal shelving against a pegboard wall in a hardware store at night, lit from below by one warm desk lamp. On the shelves, ordinary hardware items, each with a manila paper tag tied on with string, handwritten. A wind-up kitchen timer, a rain gauge, a bath plug, a rake, a coil of rope, a plunger. At the top corner, one older tag, yellowed, blank. Warm and shadowed. Documentary, not spooky. No faces, no dolls, no masks, no mirrors."

## 9. The ice machine

Left of the desk, against the wall between the counter and the front door. A chest-style commercial ice machine, dented white enamel gone cream, older than Teddy. A hand-lettered cardboard sign taped to the lid: ICE. HONOR JAR. A glass jar beside it with coins in it. A metal scoop on a hook.

It drops a load of ice at irregular intervals. The sound is a bucket of gravel on a tin roof, and it is mixed at dialogue level, never above. It is the show's clock, its drum, and Teddy's enemy. Nothing about it is threatening. It should look like a machine a person would buy ice from at midnight without thinking about it.

**Prompt.** "An old chest-style commercial ice machine in cream enamel with dents, against a hardware store wall at night, a hand-lettered cardboard sign taped to the lid reading ICE, a glass jar of coins beside it, a metal scoop on a hook. Lit by cool fluorescent from above and warm lamp spill from the right. Ordinary. No people."

## 10. The insert-shot style

The insert is the customer's story, shot in the customer's own place. It is the only time the show leaves the store, and it is where the show is at its spookiest, so it is where the rules are tightest.

**Light.** Always night. Always one source, and the source is in the frame or just off it: a flashlight, a porch light, a bedside lamp, a phone screen, a streetlight through a window. No fill. No blue moonlight from nowhere. The dark in the frame is ordinary dark, the kind a viewer has in their own hallway.

**Camera.** Handheld and slow, or locked off. Never fast. Never a whip. Never a push into the dark. If the camera moves, it moves at the speed of a person carrying a flashlight who is not in a hurry.

**Framing.** Eye level, or the point of view of the customer (a peephole, a window, a doorway). The haunted object is in the frame and does its one thing in the frame. The thing that makes the sound is on screen when the sound happens. The fair clue is in the same frame, in focus, before anyone at the desk names it.

**Sound.** The object's one sound, at dialogue level. Room tone under it: a fridge, a sprinkler, a far dog. No drone. No stinger. No music.

**What never appears in an insert.** A face at a window. A hand from the dark. A figure that follows anyone. A voice that says a name. A shadow that turns out to be a person meaning harm. Anything that moves toward the camera. Anything that flickers faster than three times a second.

**The replay.** At the reveal, the insert plays again, the same footage, and the clue is now lit or pointed at. It adds nothing. If the replay needs a new shot, the insert was wrong.

**Prompt for an insert still (no people).** "A residential front porch at night seen through a door peephole, fisheye distortion, lit by one porch light. An empty doormat. A doorbell button beside the door frame. Across the quiet street, a house with one upstairs window lit, and a hand-lettered poster board propped inside the window. Still, ordinary, slightly uneasy. No people. No faces."
