# IP check: the four winning slate titles and premises (D-015)

Agent: compliance-gate. Date: 2026-09-19. Phase 4 input to the show bibles.

Assets under check: the four tournament winners in `projects/04-writers-room/out/tournament/concepts.md` (A2, H2, W5, L1), with judge notes in `projects/04-writers-room/out/tournament/results.md`.

## Ratings key

- CLEAR: no same or confusingly similar title found, and no copied expression found. The bible proceeds under the distance rules listed.
- REVIEW: a real neighbor exists. The bible proceeds under mandatory distance rules, and counsel or the Orchestrator decides the named question before any public asset uses the title.
- BLOCK: stop the asset. Only counsel can lift it.

Eight ratings: 6 CLEAR, 2 REVIEW, 0 BLOCK.

## Method

1. Title search. For each title, web search for the exact phrase across TV, film, web series, podcasts, books, games, apps, and channels. Then a second pass with the phrase alone and a third with format words removed.
2. Trademark search. The proxy blocked USPTO, Justia Trademarks, and Trademarkia direct pages. Web search excerpts for each mark in caps with "trademark" returned no registration for any of the four. This is not a clearance. Counsel runs the direct USPTO search on any title that goes public.
3. Premise search. For each concept, search for existing shows with the same engine: judge-parent family sitcoms, school-bus kid ensembles, reenactment comedies, haunted-object anthologies. Named results are read against the concept's characters, set, devices, and sample seeds.
4. Window comparison. Each concept is compared against the 1990s block shows named in `projects/01-market/out/evidence-h2-h4-history.md` and the well-known kids' and family shows of the 1993 to 1998 window that share a setting or device.
5. Alternate check. Any proposed alternate title gets the same title search before it is proposed.

Limits. IMDb, Wikipedia, YouTube, comedy.co.uk, and the trademark databases were blocked by the egress proxy. Facts below rest on search excerpts from those pages, not on the pages themselves. Any fact that a bible relies on should be confirmed by counsel or the Orchestrator from the primary page.

## A2. Kitchen Court

### Title: CLEAR

Comparison set: "Kitchen Court" exact; "Kitchen" titles (Kitchen, The Kitchen, Kitchen Confidential, Kitchen Casino, Cutthroat Kitchen, Food Court Wars); "Court" titles (Night Court 1984 to 1992 and 2023 to 2025, Kids' Court 1988 to 1989, Family Court with Judge Penny, Teen Court inside Overruled!); Roblox and app listings; trademark excerpts.

Result. No show, film, web series, podcast, book, game, app, or channel titled "Kitchen Court" surfaced in any pass. "X Court" is a common title family for court comedies and court shows, and "Kitchen" is a common food-show word, but the pair together is distinctive. No KITCHEN COURT mark surfaced in trademark excerpts. One note for the window comparison: Nickelodeon aired "Kids' Court" from September 1988 to 1989, a kids' grievance show with a "Judge-o-meter" that measured audience cheering; it is an alliterative K-word plus "Court" title in the kids' space. It ended after 26 episodes before the window opened, and the words differ, so the title stays CLEAR. Counsel should confirm no live mark or revival before the title goes public.

### Premise: CLEAR

Comparison set and what was compared:
- Judging Amy (CBS, 1999 to 2005). Drama. A family court judge moves home with her daughter and lives with her own mother, a social worker. Compared on: judge mother, grandmother in the house, kids. Difference: drama, no home trials, no referee, no small claims.
- Overruled! (Family Channel Canada, 2009 to 2011). Kids' sitcom. High school disputes go to an after-school Teen Court with a student judge and student lawyers. Compared on: kids as litigants, a formal court for small disputes. Difference: school setting, no parent judge, no family.
- Kids' Court (Nickelodeon, 1988 to 1989). Kids mailed in grievances, two kids argued each side, a cheering meter decided. Compared on: kids' disputes as cases. Difference: studio show, audience vote, viewer mail.
- Judge Mom (2021). Court show with a mother judge persona. Compared on: "mom as judge" framing. Difference: real disputes, no family, no scripted comedy.
- Night Court (NBC). Workplace court sitcom with a judge lead. Compared on: judge-led comedy, bailiff and clerk roles. Difference: workplace, adult cases, no family home.

Result. No existing show tries family disputes at a kitchen table with a small claims judge mother, a retired referee grandmother, a party-rental father, and a six-year-old bailiff. The judge-parent category that Judge X named is a mechanic, not an expression. The Orchestrator's D-015 note asked this check to confirm distance; it is confirmed on the current concept text.

Distance the bible must keep:
1. No case in the show may mirror a Judging Amy plot, and Bea is never a social worker, court employee, or lawyer. She is a referee, and only a referee.
2. No cheering meter, audience vote, or viewer-submitted grievance decides any verdict (also STANDARDS 2.10). Renata rules. Bea calls fouls.
3. No bailiff catchphrase, night-shift setting, or court staff ensemble that echoes Night Court. Pip's bailiff bit is a six-year-old announcing people at a kitchen door.
4. The wooden spoon gavel, the fridge docket, the whistle, and the bounce house courtroom are the show's own devices. Keep them and do not add a gavel-and-bench set.
5. Cross-examination of a parent lands as affection (STANDARDS 3.2), which also keeps the show away from adversarial court-show tone.

## H2. Long Route

### Title: CLEAR

Comparison set: "Long Route" exact; "Long Way Round" and its sequels (Sky One and Apple TV motorcycle documentaries); "The Long Road Home"; "The Short History of the Long Road"; podcasts, albums, games.

Result. One exact match: "Long Route" (2016), a short drama directed by Karen Natalia Riveros Gómez, listed on IMDb as tt6218532. No series, distribution footprint, or trademark surfaced. A single short film title with no market presence does not block a series title, and the phrase is descriptive. The "Long Way" documentary family differs in words and audience. Title stays CLEAR. Counsel notes the short in the USPTO pass.

### Premise: REVIEW

Comparison set and what was compared:
- School Bus (CBBC sitcom, UK). Takes place entirely on a school bus going to and from school. Central character is twelve-year-old Noah Beckett. The kids catch up and tell each other stories from the day's events. The bus is divided into three social sections: popular kids at the back, "inbetweeners" in the middle, well-behaved kids at the front. Compared on: bus-only setting, the day retold on the ride, back-row social map. Overlap: the engine sentence "school happens off screen, the bus is where it gets processed" describes both shows.
- Bus Life (Disney Channel UK, 2003 to 2004). Thirteen five-minute episodes set solely on the top deck of a school bus, four friends, named cliques, fourth-wall address. Compared on: bus-only setting, cliques, to-and-from school. Overlap: contained set and ride-home comedy.
- School Bus Diaries (YouTube web series, 2016 to 2017). Kids and teens riding the bus of a "sweet elderly driver, Mama Bev" (Lauren Chapin) face challenges. Compared on: a warm older female driver as the adult anchor. Overlap: driver as secret lead.
- The Magic School Bus (PBS, 1994 onward, inside the window). Teacher Ms. Frizzle takes eight students on magic field trips. Compared on: a female adult and a school bus with a fixed kid cast. Difference: animated, science, magic, teacher not driver.
- The Last Bus (BBC, 2022). Kids on a school bus in a sci-fi road story. Difference: genre, quest structure.

Result. No single show matches Long Route, but three separate shows already own its three main parts: the bus-only setting with the day retold (School Bus, Bus Life), and the beloved older female driver (School Bus Diaries). The rural 48-minute route, the stop-order seating, the back-row parliament, the driver-grandson pair, and the bus code are original. The rating is REVIEW because the bible must make the original parts carry the show, and a re-check of the bible is required before any public asset. This matches the Orchestrator's D-015 note that school must be on screen through windows and stops, not only retold.

Distance the bible must keep (mandatory):
1. No social map by clique zones (popular back, quiet front, "inbetweeners" middle). Long Route's map is assigned seats by stop order, renegotiated by the parliament. Never label rows by popularity.
2. No "day retold" as the sole engine. Every episode shows school or the county through at least one stop, window, breakdown, substitute route, or field location. Stops are recurring sets with named residents.
3. Frances is not a "Mama" figure. She is dry, rule-bound, and eavesdrops. She has a grandson on the bus who is mortified. No hugs-at-the-wheel warmth as her main note.
4. No fourth-wall address to camera (Bus Life).
5. Nothing from the animated window show: no magic, no field trips, no transforming bus, no teacher at the wheel, no lizard, no "seatbelts" catchphrase, no red-curled driver in patterned dresses. Frances's look and voice must not read as that character. The bible should also decide whether the name Frances sits too near that character's surname sound; this check flags it as low risk, not a fail.
6. The parliament, the bus code, the Tuesday window vote, and the last-ride-in-reverse-order are the show's own. Build on those.

## W5. Recent History Society

### Title: CLEAR

Comparison set: "Recent History Society" exact; "Recent History" as a title; real historical societies; the Society for the History of Recent Social Science (HISRESS, academic); "Sketch History" (ZDF sketch series, 2015 to 2025); "The History of Sketch Comedy" (Audible, 2021); comedy history podcasts (The Dollop, Timesuck, Oh What A Time); the 1990s campfire storytelling club named "Midnight Society."

Result. No show, podcast, book, game, or channel titled "Recent History Society" or "Recent History" surfaced. "Society" is a generic club word. The only window neighbor is the "Midnight Society," the framing club of Nickelodeon's Saturday-block anthology (1991 to 1996, revived 1999 to 2000), which is a different title and a different genre. Title stays CLEAR.

### Premise: CLEAR

Comparison set and what was compared:
- Funny or Die, "Captain America: Civil War Reenactors" (2016 short). Two pool-supply employees reenact a film in parking lots. Compared on: reenactors as comic figures. Difference: film reenactment, mockumentary style.
- Key and Peele, "If Civil War Reenactments Were Honest." Compared on: reenactor sketch. Difference: real war, satire of period reenactment.
- The Reenactment (2023). Scenes from FOX series reenacted in various ways. Compared on: the word and the device. Difference: reenacts existing TV scenes.
- Sketch History (ZDF, 2015 to 2025). Sketches about world history. Compared on: sketch format, history. Difference: real history, satire.
- Detectorists (BBC Four, 2014 to 2022). Hobby club sitcom. Compared on: club-of-enthusiasts comedy. Difference: metal detecting, no reenactment, no family core.
- Midnight Society (Nickelodeon Saturday block, in the window). A club of kids meets weekly at a secret campfire, a member says "Submitted for the approval of the Midnight Society, I call this story," throws dust in the fire, and the story plays. Compared on: a weekly society meeting that stages a story. Difference: horror, campfire, secret woods, ritual opener.

Result. No show reenacts events from the last seven days. The last-week rule, the panel of historians who disagree, the printed program, the grandfather-and-granddaughter core, and the "no real event" rule are original. The concept already excludes mockumentary style. CLEAR.

Distance the bible must keep:
1. Never reenact a real event, real news, or a real person, in any form (STANDARDS 2.8 and this check). Every event is invented and local.
2. No Civil War or named-war reenactor jokes, no period-uniform gags, no "honest reenactment" satire. The costumes are for "last Tuesday."
3. No campfire, no secret location, no ritual opening line, no dust in a fire, no membership initiation. The Society meets in a lit community hall with folding chairs and a printed program.
4. No talking-head interviews or camera-aware mockumentary device (already in the concept text; keep it in the bible).
5. Do not pair "Society" with "Midnight" or any night-time or secret-club framing in taglines, bumpers, or metadata.

## L1. The Night Desk

### Title: REVIEW

Comparison set: "The Night Desk" exact; "Night Desk" exact; "The Night Agent" (Netflix, 2023 onward, and the Matthew Quirk novel, whose premise starts at a "night action desk"); "The Night Clerk" (2020 film); "Night Editor" (DuMont anthology, 1954); "Night Desk" by George Ryga (novel, Goodreads listing); "Welcome to Night Vale" (podcast and novels); trademark excerpts.

Result. An active YouTube channel at youtube.com/@TheNightDesk describes itself as modern horror stories told in first person, new videos weekly, "fiction that feels real." That is the same title, in horror fiction, on the video platform class this block plans to use. The handle @TheNightDesk is taken. The Ryga novel and the 1954 anthology are dormant. "The Night Agent" is a strong "The Night ___" title in a thriller lane and adds crowding. No NIGHT DESK mark surfaced in trademark excerpts, but the databases were blocked. Rating: REVIEW. The named question for counsel or the Orchestrator: keep the title and accept a same-name horror channel on the same platform with the handle gone, or retitle now before any trailer, page, or metadata uses it.

Proposed alternates (both passed a title search with no same-title show, podcast, film, book, or band found):
1. The Returns Desk. Says the set and the engine in three words. Nearest found titles are film-review podcasts with "Returns" (The Late Returns Film Review, Diminishing Returns), none confusing.
2. Two Receipts. The trailer's closing line and the show's attitude in two words. Nearest found titles are "The Receipts Podcast" and "We Have the Receipts," relationship and reality podcasts, not confusing.

### Premise: CLEAR

Comparison set and what was compared:
- Friday the 13th: The Series (syndication, 1987 to 1990). Two cousins and a friend run an antiques store and recover cursed objects a dead uncle sold under a pact, locking them in a vault. Compared on: one store, one object per episode. Difference: objects are truly cursed and dangerous, they leave the store, a vault and a pact drive the series.
- The Haunted Museum (2021, scripted anthology). Each episode dramatizes a relic from a real Las Vegas museum. Compared on: object-per-episode anthology. Difference: real relics, real threat, adult horror.
- Deadly Possessions (2016) and The Haunted Objects Podcast (2022 onward). Real "haunted" items and their owners. Compared on: object stories told to a keeper. Difference: nonfiction, no comedy, no mundane reveal.
- Goosebumps (Fox, 1995 to 1998, inside the window). Many episodes turn on one object: a rubber Halloween mask, an antique cuckoo clock, an instant camera, a ventriloquist dummy. Compared on: the spooky object as engine. Difference: the object is really haunted; the shows end on a twist, not a safe reveal.
- Are You Afraid of the Dark? (Nickelodeon Saturday block, inside the window). Hosted anthology with a club frame. Compared on: hosted anthology mechanic. Difference: campfire frame, real scares.
- Creeped Out (CBBC, 2017 onward). Kids' anthology with a masked observer figure, "The Curious." Compared on: kids' anthology with a recurring frame figure. Difference: real supernatural, masked narrator.
- Hardware (ITV, 2003 to 2004). Sitcom in a London hardware store. Compared on: hardware store as set. Difference: adult workplace, day shift, no anthology.
- Home Improvement (ABC, 1991 to 1999, on the Friday block in the window). Family sitcom with a tool show inside it. Compared on: hardware and tools as a comic world. Difference: no store, no night, no objects.

Result. No anthology uses a hardware returns desk, an overnight clerk who requires a story instead of a receipt, or a rule that every object is explained by a kindness or a mistake. The hosted anthology and the object-per-episode shape are mechanics. The mundane-reveal rule inverts the cursed-object genre rather than copying it. CLEAR, with the distance below.

Distance the bible must keep (the three expressive choices that must differ from the known haunted-object anthologies, plus window rules):
1. No object is ever really haunted, cursed, or dangerous. No vault, no pact, no collector who must recover items, no "the object must be contained" plot. Objects go home with their owners, explained (this is the opposite of Friday the 13th: The Series and The Haunted Museum).
2. No object from the window anthology's famous episodes: no Halloween mask that bonds to a face, no cuckoo clock that moves time, no camera that predicts harm, no ventriloquist dummy that speaks. Keep to hardware stock: doorbells, ladders, paint, gnomes, keys, chimes, bulbs, mailboxes.
3. No frame ritual: no campfire, no club, no "submitted for approval" opener, no masked or unseen narrator who watches the customer. Marguerite is a clerk at a lit desk, and the customer tells the story.
4. No tool-show-within-a-show, no grunting handyman, no neighbor seen only over a fence. Hardison's is a store at night, not a workshop.
5. No real museum, real relic, or real ghost story as a source for any episode.
6. Keep the D-015 Orchestrator note: a recurring young character at the desk, so the slot has a kid to root for. Teddy, 16, can carry this if he is at the desk and not only in the aisles.
